Fallacy and evidential diagnostic: where the argument directed at my position fails, and how my own argument differs

In plain English, this section asks a simple question at each step: does the reason being offered actually support the conclusion it is being used to support, against the position I actually hold? That is what the technical fallacy names below are tracking.

The first six concern representation, relevance, logical alternatives, presupposition, and semantic burden. The seventh concerns evidential reach, which matters because the argument moves from metaphysical interpretation into claims about what causation, experience, valuation, and nature actually are.

1. Target substitution / straw-man structure is the upstream failure. The version distributed to subscribers converted suspension of judgment into “jettisoning” experience and later into a “supposedly agnostic nonexperiential account.” Matt’s own erratum acknowledges that he “collapse[d] the two-way conditional structure” and my “explicit agnosticism.” The chronology places the relevant distinctions upstream of that article: in my reply in the comment thread beneath Matt’s Spiral Genesis, Continued, three days before publication, I explicitly kept agnosticism about primitive intrinsic character as a third rival, stated that ablation fails if removing P materially changes C, and moved failed subtraction to whole-rival reconstruction. Walton and Macagno’s peer-reviewed analysis of misquotation and straw-man structures is directly relevant because distorted reporting changes an interlocutor’s commitments and therefore changes what burdens can legitimately be assigned to the position. The same structure recurs later when my claim that debate continued after the concession while repair remained incomplete is answered as though I had claimed Matt debated instead of conceding the error.

2. Much of the rebuttal commits ignoratio elenchi, or wrong conclusion, relative to the actual point under dispute. The article argues that Whiteheadian experience is not detachable, that real causation is more than bare succession, that a merely formal description does not exhaust concrete actuality, that a positive theory of primitive nonexperience would owe an emergence account, and that a crude evolutionary switch would be explanatorily weak. I already grant those premises. Even if each argument succeeds, defeating those thinner positions does not answer the live question: what warrants the universal identity of concrete causal actuality with feeling over serious target-preserving whole rivals? Douglas Walton’s peer-reviewed classification of ignoratio elenchi as the wrong-conclusion or missing-the-point fallacy fits this structure: an argument can establish something true and still fail to establish the proposition actually at issue.

3. The shift from agnosticism to nonexperience is an illicit epistemic negation shift. The revised article now recognizes the distinction when it says brute emergence becomes a problem if agnosticism is eventually resolved in favor of genuinely nonexperiential process. Yet the surrounding reasoning still assigns the agnostic position the liabilities of a “blank,” missing actuality, missing causal influence, missing valuation, and ultimately a “wholly feelingless” cosmos. Formally, ?X is being made to carry the burdens of not-X. Woods and Walton’s classic analysis of ad ignorantiam identifies precisely the underlying epistemic error: confusing lack of confirming evidence with disconfirming evidence, and treating the difference through an illicit negation shift. Withholding assent to a universal experiential identity does not supply the contrary identity.

4. “Minimal interiority or bare succession” is a false dilemma. The article says that pressing on inheritance, uptake, response, and receptivity yields two options: either the verbs reveal “a minimal interiority,” or they collapse into “bare succession.” That pair is not exhaustive. Powers and process theories supply a third family of live possibilities in which causation is productive, directed, dispositional, relational, and non-Humean while the universal experiential identity remains unsettled. Matt later made the non-exhaustiveness explicit himself when he allowed that “a dispositional account of causality” could in principle render influence intelligible without his character/occurrence distinction. Macagno’s peer-reviewed analysis of false dichotomy applies when two alternatives are presented as exhausting a space that contains additional live options.

The “blank” question carries a presuppositional burden shift. “What fills the blank left by bracketing experience?” already presupposes that bracketing the disputed experiential identity leaves a blank. “Actuality itself goes missing when you withhold experience” then treats that presupposition as though it were the result of the comparison. But the target under comparison already contains concrete occurrence, real causal efficacy, process, relation, inheritance, and receptivity; what remains open is the further identity of those realities with felt inheritance. Macagno’s analysis of presuppositional fallacies is especially apt here: an unearned presupposition can be inserted as common ground and thereby reverse the burden of proof. The “blank” has to be established before I can inherit a burden to fill it.

The bridge I am asking Matt for was already contested in Whitehead scholarship

The distinction above also changes how I understand the history of the objection. The question I am pressing has a substantial history inside and around process philosophy. Several scholars and critics have challenged the move from the experiential character disclosed in some cases to an experiential characterization of actuality generally, or have challenged whether the experiential and prehensive machinery receives evidential support independent of the system that employs it.

The historical record treats the universalization from experienced causation to experiential actuality as a substantive philosophical bridge requiring warrant.

Arthur E. Murphy’s 1941 essay in Paul Arthur Schilpp’s The Philosophy of Alfred North Whitehead is an especially direct antecedent. It was an invited critical contribution to a major scholarly volume rather than an anonymously refereed journal article. Murphy’s complaint is that a claim to ultimate metaphysical identification “needs more warrant than the initial experience can itself provide.” He then asks, very specifically, how Whitehead establishes that no actual entity can be “vacuously actual,” meaning devoid of subjective immediacy or feeling. Later, when discussing the move from what is enjoyed in our own experience to the inner actuality attributed elsewhere, Murphy argues that the bridge depends on a speculative identification between what is most concretely or aesthetically enjoyed in the human case and what is taken to be existentially pervasive. In the surrounding pages he preserves substantial relations between experience and nature while resisting the further universalization of specifically experiential, aesthetic, or emotional character beyond the conditions that independently warrant it.

Murphy also anticipates a structural pattern relevant to my exchange with Matt. He warns that once the speculative identification is treated as the measure of what is ultimately actual, rejecting its universalization can be redescribed as denying actuality, value, life, or meaning altogether. Murphy says “The argument is simply working in the reverse direction”: if the favored characteristic is not ultimately pervasive, the critic is made to look as though it is not real anywhere. The parallel here is specific: my suspension of the universal identity was repeatedly redescribed through a “bloodless,” “wholly feelingless” cosmos even while my positive commitments included concrete causal process, value-bearing organisms, and later experience. The historical comparison therefore sharpens the distinction between withholding the universal identification and denying the locally manifest phenomenon.

William Ernest Hocking pressed a related but distinct worry in the same volume. Hocking accepted Whitehead’s motive for refusing to derive concrete change, novelty, and subjectivity from primitives that exclude them in advance, but argued that the reverse strategy risks putting the phenomenon to be explained into the primitive description and then treating its presence there as an explanation. His criticism is easy to overstate: every metaphysics eventually reaches primitives, and a primitive need not be derivable from something more basic. The local question that survives is whether calling causal inheritance, receptivity, or creative advance feeling adds a discriminating commitment or merely redescribes the processual structure already granted. Hocking’s other point, later taken up by Hugh R. King, was that the actual occasion should be treated as a hypothesis whose conceptual value has to be earned rather than as a datum that can be secured simply by pointing to its place in the scheme.

Whitehead did not publish a reply resolving Murphy’s or Hocking’s objections. That silence should not be treated as a concession. A contemporary Nature review of the Schilpp volume records the reason: “Unfortunately a serious illness has prevented him from doing so.” The same notice reports Whitehead’s suggestion that, had health and age allowed, the volume might instead have led to “devoting many years to rewriting [his] previous works.” We therefore do not have a late Whitehead rebuttal showing that these bridge and explanatory-primitive objections had been answered and dissolved.

The issue then entered the peer-reviewed Process Studies literature directly. John J.
