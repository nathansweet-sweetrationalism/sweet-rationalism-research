Matthew Segall, participatory inquiry, universal prehension, and how a qualified argument becomes a public caricature.


One of my favorite lines comes from Philip K. Dick. It has the useful quality of being equally inconvenient to a theologian, a skeptic, and the person quoting it:

Related evidence and competing explanations: Matthew David Segall evidence and criticism index.

Reality is that which, when you stop believing in it, doesn't go away.

Philip K. Dick, How to Build a Universe That Doesn’t Fall Apart Two Days Later.

Dick’s line has become more demanding for me over time. Reality’s independence from my assent leaves plenty of room for me to misunderstand it. An experience can be real while my explanation of it is mistaken. A conviction I cannot shake tells me about its grip on me; it does not establish that its object exists. Nor must belief be something I can switch off on command for the question of belief-independence to make sense.

I’ve spent years as a computer information systems specialist, network engineer, and network administrator, working with complex networks. A beautifully coherent explanation of a fault can still leave a network down. The failure gives me something to investigate. Identifying its cause takes further work: comparing accounts, checking dependencies, and seeing whether a change produces the expected result. That is the habit I bring into philosophy.

Some realities also depend on shared practices. Refusing to believe in a bill rarely impresses the electric company. I increasingly value Dick’s sentence for the questions it opens: what depends on my belief, what warrants my interpretation, and what could correct it? My own framework owes answers too.

Before another explanation of what I supposedly misunderstand, here is what I actually argue.

  • Truth and inquiry. We investigate the world from inside it, through bodies, relationships, instruments, and ideas that can change what happens. We can still get the explanation wrong. I distinguish whether a claim gets something right, what reasons we have to believe it, and what authority it receives. Some facts depend on our participation; we can still misunderstand them. My framework helps assess those judgments while leaving the larger theory of truth open.
  • What I affirm. I take processes, causal capacities, organization, history, and constraints seriously. My process-and-powers account explains particular causal work; the wider framework also examines dependencies, improves questions, and asks which conclusions survive competing interpretations. PBSRR is my provisional ontology, RCF my correction method, and Sweet Rationalism my ethos. All three remain open to revision and comparison with simpler alternatives.
  • What remains open. I withhold assent to the claim that causal actuality everywhere consists in felt inheritance or Whiteheadian prehension. That leaves its truth unresolved. I claim neither a solution to consciousness nor a completed cosmology superior to Whitehead’s. Withholding belief, challenging necessity, offering a better explanation, and making progress under uncertainty each require different work. An adequate explanation without prehension challenges its necessity to that explanation, while leaving universal experience open.
  • What Matthew still needs to establish. Coherence, breadth, participation, and transformation can support a philosophical view without uniquely establishing it. We still need to ask which commitments produce the gains, how far the evidence travels, and what favors divine valuation or a particular divine source. Those questions remain for both of us.
  • The ethical and computational questions. What good does divine remembrance bring the original sufferer? What identifies the source of a transformative encounter? Which features of a computational system bear on consciousness, and how does it affect human agency regardless? Each question needs its own evidence and a clear account of whose capacities or welfare are at stake.|
  • The public record. Several alleged misunderstandings conflict with qualifications already in my articles. Matthew also offers useful clarifications, substantive disagreements, corrections, errata, and concessions that deserve credit. A public comment below shows one reader repeating and strengthening some of the criticisms. That concerns me regardless of Matt's intent. My own attributions and arguments face the same scrutiny.

I want to make the disagreement easy to check, acknowledge how Matthew’s challenges have helped me develop the argument, and give future replies something more definite to answer than a silhouette of an obstinate skeptic.

Truth without an observer outside the universe

In his October 26 response, Matthew suggests that we may disagree more deeply about philosophy and truth. He writes:

Correspondence is real enough, but I regard it as derivative of a more primordial participation of knower and known.

He also writes:

We aren't fully constituted subjects standing outside an already finished universe, inspecting it and attempting to produce increasingly accurate internal pictures of what is happening out there.

I agree. My Provisional, Boundary- and Scale-Relative Realism, or PBSRR, already says:

Science can be perspectival, agent-mediated, and model-plural without making truth arbitrary.

My Ethos also says:

How we describe and treat one another can help create the conditions in which people exercise agency, learn, or recover confidence.

I distinguish a knower, what is known, and the activity of knowing within an ongoing worldly process. The investigator’s capacities depend on a body, history, surroundings, and often other people and instruments. Those relations can shape what becomes available to inquiry and can change the investigator and the target. Asking whether an account gets something right is an activity within that process. It introduces no observer outside it.

Those distinctions still matter. The target’s existence need not depend on anyone investigating it, and participation need not make the participants’ explanations correct. Relational dependence, different roles, and possible error can coexist. My framework asks which dependencies actually hold and what their boundaries are.

So I can agree that knowing depends on participation while asking what “more primordial” establishes. Is correspondence derivative in its development, its justification, its truth conditions, or the constitution of actuality itself? If Matthew means that knowing arises through situated relations, we already share that commitment. If he means those relations universally consist in felt inheritance, the disputed identification returns. That further claim still needs its argument.

The risk in using this contrast against my position is that it supplies the separated spectator I have already rejected, then offers participation as the remedy. I am asking how a process involving knower, known, and knowing produces a warranted account, and why one ontological interpretation earns preference. If my argument nevertheless relies on an independently constituted spectator, show me where.

Matthew introduces the spectator contrast and suggests a deeper disagreement. He stops short of calling me a naive correspondence theorist. The public commenter goes further, accusing me of reducing truth to copying a finished external reality. That escalation matters to the public example below.

By truth here, I mean whether a claim gets right what it says is the case. By warrant, I mean the reasons that justify accepting it, reasons we may later revise. Calling something true can also confer authority in a conversation or institution. Whether it deserves that authority is another question. These distinctions let me ask what happened, how we know, and whose account gets accepted without assuming those questions always have the same answer.

Our senses and interpretations can be mistaken. When I call an account provisionally true on the best evidence available to me, I am describing a revisable judgment. That does not make my confidence the condition of its correctness or impose an obligation on everyone else to accept it. What the claim says is the case, how well I can establish it, and what authority others should grant my judgment remain distinguishable.

A modest correspondence commitment fits that view. We can ask whether a description gets something right from our position inside nature, using a vocabulary we may later revise. The Stanford Encyclopedia’s The Correspondence Theory of Truth describes a diverse family of views. Its Truth entry also discusses pragmatist accounts that retain answerability to an independent world. “Correspondence” and “participation” can coexist.

Mathematical proof, historical reconstruction, causal investigation, descriptions of experience, and ethical reasoning also justify claims in different ways. I leave the larger disputes about truthmakers, propositions, future contingents, and moral truth open. My framework helps assess warrant across those questions.

Wittgenstein’s attention to how words work in Philosophical Investigations helps here. Saying an account is true, calling someone a truth-teller, and describing an institution’s accepted truths can do different jobs. I want to know which use an objection challenges before deciding what follows from it.

Here is the distinction I use in PBSRR:

Maintain three different judgments: whether a proposition is true, whether its current warrant earns adoption, and whether investigating it remains useful.

I can find a proposal worth pursuing, decline to adopt it, and leave its ultimate truth unresolved. Those judgments tell us different things about where the inquiry stands.

The Ethos makes the related point:

Practical usefulness, predictive success, explanatory compression, successful intervention, coordination, and resilience can all be evidence-bearing achievements. None is a definition of truth, and none by itself licenses an ontology.

I describe those achievements as “evidence-bearing” because they can support particular claims. Successful intervention can support a causal claim. A practice can improve attention or cooperation. A new concept can reveal a neglected relation. I want to know what each achievement supports and how far that support reaches.

Matthew writes:

Truth is also something we participate in bringing about.

We certainly can bring some truths about. We establish a community, make an agreement, or build an institution. Some facts exist through those activities and the recognition that sustains them. John Searle’s Social Ontology: Some Basic Principles distinguishes that dependence from whether our judgments about those facts are correct. An agreement can depend on a shared practice while I remain mistaken about what it requires.

Participation needs that specificity. Taking part in inquiry, causally changing an outcome, and helping constitute a practice are different relations. So is endorsing an explanation of them. We might create a community and still misidentify what sustains it or credit divine intervention for an improvement brought about by changed practices. The participation is real; its explanation still needs assessing.

Terence McKenna puts the challenge memorably in Countdown Into Complexity, at about seventeen minutes:

The truth does not require your participation in order to exist. Bullshit does!

My reading is a qualified observation about social persistence: truth may obtain without our endorsement, while bullshit commonly needs circulation or uptake to retain a meaningful role in human society. A piece of bullshit can remain archived without an active audience, and some true social facts depend on participation. Those qualifications keep the aphorism from becoming a universal metaphysical law. Our endorsement cannot, by itself, make an account correct; where belief helps constitute a fact, we need to identify that dependence.

McKenna goes further in the surrounding talk, treating belief itself as a surrender of freedom. I’m willing to believe what the reasons support and revise when they change.

Matthew also says the past is settled and the future is not. That adds a position about time and possibility to the fact that inquiry happens within nature. Several rival metaphysics allow our actions to help produce later states. A new understanding, a newly available course of action, and a change in what is fundamentally possible still require different arguments. If the account needs objective indeterminacy or genuinely new possibilities, we should examine those commitments.

Matthew already relies on enough shared ground for that examination. There is a difference between what I wrote and what someone attributes to me. We can be mistaken. Transformation can leave a vision’s truth unresolved, and experiencing a presence can leave its source uncertain. We can use those agreements to assess an inference while our cosmologies remain unfinished.

What follows from having a concept of reality?

My exchange with the Philosopher Michael Kowalik about Dick’s aphorism makes the distinction more exact. Michael writes:

I am saying that reality is identical with the concept of reality, and it persists only to the extent that someone believes it.

I asked:

What establishes that reality equals its concept? I distinguish ideas from what they represent, not an “idea that is not an idea.” Conceiving something as predating conception doesn’t mean this conception existed then.

His subsequent reply begins:

If what you mean by reality is not identical with your concept of reality, then you do not mean what you mean: contradiction.

There are two questions inside that sentence. What conceptual content am I expressing, and what am I trying to refer to? If “what you mean” denotes my conceptual content throughout, I have not asserted that this content differs from itself. If it denotes the intended referent, Michael still needs to establish why distinguishing it from a concept entails self-nonidentity. His conditional asserts that connection without deriving it from the commitments I stated.

My claim is that a concept can represent something without being numerically identical to it. That rejects a requirement of identity for every instance of representation; it does not settle every identity claim or assert that concepts and their targets are independent. I can affirm a representation–referent distinction while asking what establishes Michael’s universal identification. Nor does conceiving something as earlier require the present act of conception to have occurred earlier. The time described within a thought and the time of thinking it are different questions.

Frege’s distinction between sense and reference helps specify the problem. Different ways of presenting something can share a referent, as in the morning-star and evening-star example. The distinction therefore supplies no automatic argument for ontological separation. It does show why differences among descriptions do not, by themselves, establish differences among their targets.

A representation can be selective without being inaccurate about what it asserts. Getting a specified feature right does not require reproducing or exhausting the target. Self-reference is possible too: a sentence can refer to itself. I therefore make no rule that representation and referent must always differ. The disputed inference is that intelligibility or accuracy requires their numerical identity.

The move from conditions of conceiving to the constitution of what is conceived resembles one argument toward idealism. In The Problems of Philosophy, Chapter IV, Bertrand Russell challenges a related inference in Berkeley: the mental character of apprehending does not by itself establish the mental character of its object. That objection targets an inference; it does not refute every idealist account.

A correction also reaches my own criticism. Hornsby’s identity theory of truth (1997), developed in discussion with McDowell, identifies true thinkables with facts while distinguishing thought-content from the activity of thinking. It does not identify every psychological episode with its subject matter or turn every belief into a fact. Sullivan (2005) nevertheless argues that modern identity theories remain vulnerable to an idealist objection. This is a live dispute about the identities being proposed. The word identity alone establishes neither absurdity nor the belief-dependence Michael asserts.

The possibility of error remains relevant even when we investigate beliefs themselves. An account of my argument can be internally consistent while attributing a commitment I do not hold. Deriving a contradiction from that attribution would not establish a contradiction in my position. If the commitment is alleged to be implicit, the inference needs showing. A coherence-based explanation of accurate attribution could be offered; merely observing that both accounts are beliefs leaves that task unfinished.

Persistence also needs a specified subject. A continuing experience and continued acceptance of an interpretation of it are distinguishable possibilities. Michael’s initial reply was:

If it doesn’t go away, then you are still believing in it.

Which belief does that identify: that an experience occurs, or that its apparent object exists as first interpreted? A definition equating persistence with belief cannot independently establish that the abandoned interpretation remains accepted. Moving from my belief to someone’s belief changes the claim again. Even if the latter dependence were established, it would not establish my continued assent. The narrower persistence claim needs no proof of independence from every mind: a practice can outlast my disbelief while other people sustain it.

Michael also writes:

The crucial point is that if your concept of truth/reality is not fully reducible to sense, then it is non-sense.

In this passage, sense appears to concern intelligibility or systemic coherence, rather than infallible sensory access. If that is the standard, the argument still needs to show why my distinction fails it. Defining the permissible vocabulary within one account does not establish that a rival distinction contradicts itself. A reductio must supply that derivation. Treating the contradictory reformulation as my stated position would instead substitute the claim being criticized.

Meaning, reference, and the later replies

Michael’s later reply describes my position more directly:

You assert (as most people do) that the idea of a thing represents the real thing (which is not identical with the idea). I argue that they are necessarily identical, because the distinction implies contradiction, which renders the common idea of reality meaningless (non-sense).

That substantially identifies the distinction I defend. The criticism of this reply concerns the missing inference, rather than an accusation that its opening sentence attributes an alien position to me. He still needs to show how the distinction entails contradiction. An improved attribution can deserve credit while its proposed refutation remains unproved.

His next response reads:

What you mean by the ‘thing itself’ is just what ‘you mean’, or else it is nothing. The intended meaning is called an idea, or concept.

I grant that my expression has conceptual content. That leaves further questions: what does it purport to identify, does anything answer to it, and how accurately does the claim describe that target if it succeeds? A thought about a dragon can occur without a corresponding animal existing; a belief about an existing animal can be mistaken. The processes producing an imagined threat can also affect conduct without the threat being present. Occurrence, successful reference, accuracy, and identity cannot all be established merely by defining intended meaning as an idea.

The Stanford Encyclopedia’s Reference distinguishes acts of referring from the relations between expressions and their targets, while Nonexistent Objects surveys rival accounts of discourse about apparent objects that do not exist. These debates leave room to dispute particular semantic theories. A meaningful expression does not, by itself, settle the existence or constitution of what it purports to describe.

My reply also makes another limit explicit:

Representational accuracy does not establish ontological identity, and nonidentity does not entail ontological separability.

Separability here concerns whether the relevant things could exist independently. A representation can be a process within the reality it represents. It may depend causally or constitutively on its surroundings without being identical to the whole it concerns. Distinguishing a participant, a practice, and an account of that practice leaves their dependencies to investigate. It does not assert two disconnected realms.

The demand for a contradiction must preserve those commitments. If an argument derives a conflict from them, I need to address it. If the conflict appears only after adding the identity I asked Michael to justify, that additional premise remains part of the dispute. My examples establish the limited failures of inference they illustrate; they do not complete a theory of reference or refute every idealism.

These quotations concern the Dick exchange. My separate discussion of McKenna is a different thread. The comparison turns on the words posted in each, rather than a judgment about Michael’s motives or a classification of his entire philosophy.

Who gets to call something true?

There is a serious objection worth keeping: the language of truth can hide the assumptions and relationships that give an account its authority. People can inherit a premise, forget its history, and begin treating disagreement as a defect in the person who questions it. My framework has to remain vulnerable to that criticism too.

In Truth and Power, Michel Foucault examines how institutions, procedures, sanctions, and authorized speakers organize what a society accepts as true. That reaches into inquiry itself: who can ask a question, which experiences become evidence, and which explanations receive a hearing. I take that as a reason to examine my standards and their effects, including whom they exclude. It does not, on its own, settle the accuracy of a particular account.

Roland Barthes’s Myth Today identifies a related move: a historically produced arrangement comes to appear natural and inevitable. That gives me a useful question to ask of any allegedly necessary category: what makes it necessary, and what have we merely become accustomed to? Its history can expose an unsupported assumption. History alone will not establish that the assumption is false.

Both insights matter to this exchange. Calling a disputed interpretation a correction can affect how readers approach the disagreement. So can my choice of what counts as evidence or an adequate answer. We need to examine those effects alongside the passages and arguments at issue. An account of how a claim acquired authority can reveal a source of reliability or a source of distortion; the particular connection needs showing.

Harry Frankfurt’s On Bullshit sharpens another distinction. On his account, bullshit involves indifference to whether an assertion is true. It can happen to be accurate, just as an earnest, carefully investigated claim can turn out false. Truth concerns what the assertion gets right; Frankfurt’s category concerns how it is produced. An unsupported claim gives us grounds to challenge its support, without yet establishing the speaker’s indifference.

That also limits my own preferred test. An account’s willingness to face correction matters to its trustworthiness; it cannot serve as a definition of truth or guarantee that its present answer is right. The point is to make mistakes discoverable and consequential. The framework has to do that work in practice.

Geoffrey Bowker and Susan Leigh Star’s Sorting Things Out makes the stakes of classification concrete: categories can organize access, recognition, and exclusion. Helen Longino’s account of critical inquiry asks more of a community than permission to object. Criticism needs a venue, publicly available standards, a fair hearing, and uptake capable of changing the inquiry. I take that to mean that the categories in my framework must themselves be open to challenge, especially by people whose lives they help organize. Can someone contest the terms of the assessment, or only submit an answer inside the boxes I supplied? An invitation to criticize means little if the criticism goes safely into a drawer.

What disagreement leaves us able to do

Disagreement about ultimate foundations need not prevent every shared decision. John Rawls’s idea of an overlapping consensus, also examined in Iason Gabriel’s work on alignment, allows people to support shared political principles for different philosophical or religious reasons. Amartya Sen’s The Possibility of Social Choice shows why partial comparisons and a broader informational basis can support decisions without a complete ranking of everything. Neither approach guarantees agreement. They give us alternatives to waiting for one final philosophy before acting.

We also need to distinguish what people value from what they ought to value. Shalom Schwartz and colleagues’ cross-national research on individual values investigates motivational patterns and conflicts. Establishing such patterns does not establish which priorities should govern us. Hume’s question about the move from is to ought remains a useful check on that inference. I affirm care, resistance to domination, and a meaningful right to challenge consequential decisions. Those are ethical commitments I owe reasons for, including when they conflict; they do not fall out of a description of persistence or intelligence.

Gabriel also distinguishes following instructions, satisfying preferences, and serving interests or values. Those goals can diverge. Elinor Ostrom’s work on polycentric governance supplies institutional possibilities worth investigating: rules fitted to particular settings, participation in shaping them, accountable monitoring, and accessible ways to resolve conflict. Applying those lessons to computational governance would require further work. A framework can help make disagreements and consequences legible; whether people can use it, and what incentives shape its use, are further questions.

For my own proposals, I want the decision rule, the authority to apply it, and the grounds for challenging it made explicit. Protective intent alone cannot authorize coercion. A proposed intervention needs a specified harm, reasons to expect benefit, comparison with less restrictive alternatives, and limits that the affected people can contest. “For your own good” is a reason to ask who holds the keys. Assistance should remain available without requiring assent to my worldview.

The positive model I am advancing

Here is the positive account behind my suspension of the universal claim. In Where the Necessity Case for Whitehead’s Prehension Fails and Why That Makes Whitehead More Useful, Not Less, I write:

Causal production is the concrete exercise of capacities by organized processes under constraints, making a difference to the transformations that can and do occur.

I draw that account from work on productive mechanisms, powers and dispositions, causal processes, biological organization, and dependencies we can investigate through intervention. Those traditions differ, so I owe an argument for bringing their resources together.

That positive account answers a particular demand in Matthew’s criticism: explain what produces change if feeling is withheld as a premise. If withholding feeling necessarily leaves no actual process or influence to explain, then prehension is being treated as indispensable to causal intelligibility. The question is whether the alternatives really incur that loss. Their ultimate intrinsic interpretation can remain open while we investigate what they explain.

The comparison asks what concrete systems do, how their history and organization support particular capacities, and what changes when we alter the relevant processes. That gives us causal work to examine beyond the observation that equations omit experiential vocabulary.

Take a process that keeps a variable within a range through feedback. To explain it, identify what detects a difference, which pathways carry it, what changes the outcome, and when the arrangement fails. We can learn why that regulation works while leaving the constituents’ ultimate intrinsic character open. If experience contributes something indispensable to the explanation, we should be able to identify that contribution.

I’d describe my current worldview as:

one objective reality; real causal and modal constraints on possible transformations; dynamically maintained processes and patterns; and no default privileged scale or boundary.

I call that working view modal-relational process realism. PBSRR helps me decide how much confidence and scope each claim earns. When I choose a system boundary, I have to justify it for the question I’m asking. The smallest constituent and largest whole receive no automatic priority. A causal pattern can be real at one scale while a deeper account remains unfinished.

That one reality includes institutions and relationships sustained by participants. Treating them as real leaves us needing to explain their particular dependencies. It commits me to neither a world wholly independent of minds nor one wholly made by them.

I use RCF to examine those commitments and revise them. Sweet Rationalism sets out the accompanying commitments to care, fair interpretation, and room for criticism. These parts should be able to correct one another.

In the causal article, I describe what I retain this way:

D retains the process-relational, causal, organizational, and evidenced experiential content I find well supported in Whitehead and the sciences.

What I leave open is universal prehension: the further claim that causal actuality throughout nature consists in felt inheritance. The arguments examined have not persuaded me to adopt it.

That leaves several questions unresolved. Does causal activity in lifeless domains have subjective form or valuation? Would a demonstrated phenomenal contribution establish fundamentality, and would fundamentality establish universal distribution? Where the evidence leaves intrinsic constitution open, I leave it open too. I infer no underlying substance that is intrinsically nonexperiential.

“D plus question mark X” is shorthand for that uncertainty. To make a prediction, I still have to specify mechanisms, conditions, observation assumptions, and uncertainty. The notation supplies none of that by magic.

As I put it in the article:

By open I mean unresolved truth, not equal support for every rival.

I defend some conclusions, investigate others, and withhold those that go beyond the grounds available. Calling that indecision skips the judgments a critic would need to challenge.

I also acknowledge where the account stops:

The process-and-power accounts have their own primitives, and none derives why those powers exist in the first place.

I owe a comparison between that stopping point and the alternatives. A different name for an unexplained primitive would leave the difficulty where it was.

I like the way Carl Sagan gives wonder a place inside the inquiry. In The Burden of Skepticism, he writes:

I maintain there is much more wonder in science than in pseudoscience.

That is one of my favorites because curiosity belongs in the work. I would keep the comparative boast open to argument and ground science’s credibility in particular achievements. Its theories can be mistaken. Wonder can motivate a question, a model, or a search; what the search finds decides which expectations survive. My framework has to make room for surprising answers, including ones I initially find implausible.

What we can learn before settling the larger question

There is useful work to do while that universal identification stays open. I describe it this way:

My positive programme includes explaining particular causal dependencies, constructing comparisons, and making corrections propagate.

Understanding a mechanism tells us why an outcome occurs. We may also learn which premises an argument depends on, or discover that two accounts have been answering different questions. Each adds something to our understanding before we have a finished cosmology.

For example, I write:

If independently supported premises yield a conclusion without a disputed premise, I have established that derivation’s dependence structure.

That establishes how the argument depends on its premises. An adequate alternative can challenge explanatory necessity at its stated target. Showing that reality could exist without the disputed feature takes a further modal argument, for example a defensible countermodel. The conclusion has to match the achievement.

Sometimes the question itself needs work. Explaining a particular experience, a unified subject, a report, phenomenal character, fundamentality, and universal distribution involves several related problems. We might learn about subject formation while leaving fundamentality open. Or we might place experience throughout nature and still need to explain particular subjects and qualities. I want to know which problem a proposed answer actually advances.

We can also learn which conclusions survive competing interpretations. If a conclusion follows from independently warranted premises without a disputed premise, that premise is dispensable to the derivation. If the conclusion survives the serious rival completions examined, we have established robustness across those alternatives. Both results have a stated scope; neither, by itself, ranks the alternatives’ probabilities.

Empirical confirmation needs a further comparison. If two specified models give the evidence the same justified, nonzero likelihood, that evidence leaves their pairwise odds unchanged, although both may gain against a third model. Merely accommodating the same finding establishes no such equality. We need the predictive assumptions that make the comparison possible.

Suppose rival accounts agree about a system’s organization and the effects of particular interventions, while disagreeing about intrinsic experience. We may still establish what sustains a capacity, what damages it, and what restores it. We have narrowed the uncertainty. How much confidence that earns depends on the shared assumptions and the range of alternatives examined.

That comparison has to preserve the question each account is trying to answer. My Framework Bridge says:

Preserve analytical distinction without presupposing ontological separability; compare whole constitutive interpretations when subtraction would beg the question.

Evidence for an activity and evidence for its proposed experiential identity can be examined separately, even if both descriptions ultimately concern the same reality. Where changing a category changes the whole account, we need to compare the resulting accounts together. I cannot create a convenient shared core by deleting the very feature in dispute.

A comparison among observers needs the same care. Suppose two instruments and a human report are used to track one changing process. We need a model connecting their records, the conditions under which that connection should hold, and changes that could expose a mistaken mapping. Ronald Giere’s account of scientific representation makes the purpose and selected respects of a model’s use explicit. Agreement among differently produced records can support a specified dependency without requiring identical experiences or descriptions.

William Wimsatt’s analysis of robustness also warns that apparently independent methods can share a bias. Two instruments using one faulty reference can agree for the wrong reason. The comparison should therefore examine shared assumptions and different ways of failing. Such work can improve access while leaving ultimate constitution open. Calling an instrument an observer in this operational comparison attributes no experience or unified subjecthood to it.

This can lead somewhere new. Changing the boundary may expose a dependency we missed. Reconstructing a rival may reveal a better question. Finding a missing bridge may suggest a measurement or intervention. Each proposed advance then needs checking. My general account of causal production becomes an explanation of a particular case when it identifies the capacities, organization, constraints, and dependencies producing the outcome. Merely naming powers earns as little as merely naming prehension.

The practical stakes are easy to see. A comparison may reveal someone affected by a decision, expose a mistaken attribution that shaped later criticism, or identify a dependency worth protecting. Those are achievements we can investigate. I also spell out the limit of the examples examined so far:

The documented corrections show the method operating; its advantage over simpler methods still needs evaluation.

That limit matters when I organize claims in records or a knowledge graph. Saving a correction, retrieving it in the right context, applying it to the affected judgments, and improving a later decision are different achievements. I want observations, interpretations, rival explanations, dependencies, and reasons to revise a claim kept distinguishable. Aidan Hogan and colleagues’ survey of knowledge graphs also makes clear why the assumptions used to interpret a graph matter: an unrecorded relation need not be absent from the world. Probability estimates need grounds and an updating model; a graph does not acquire them simply by connecting its entries. Have I made an error easier to correct, or merely easier to find again?

I’m trying to understand worldly processes and improve how we investigate them. Matthew’s challenges have helped me clarify how those tasks fit together. Whether my extra procedures improve on careful ordinary philosophy and science is a question I still owe my own work.

Necessity, abduction, and what my title actually targets

Matthew worries that the title “Where the Necessity Case for Whitehead's Prehension Fails” suggests he offers a deductive demonstration. He also acknowledges:

I realize you distinguish explanatory indispensability from metaphysical necessity in the article itself.

He then emphasizes:

My arguments are abductive.

My article already says:

This is comparative warrant, not a demand for conclusive proof.

And it describes the proposed gains as:

Those are potential abductive virtues. Whether they produce a net advantage depends on the explanatory work and bridge assumptions of the complete theory.

My title names the claim that prehension is indispensable to the causal explanation under examination. Calling an argument abductive tells us how a view is being supported; it does not prevent the view from claiming that something is necessary. An inference to the best explanation can favor a theory partly because its distinctive commitment allegedly supplies what every adequate account needs. My article challenges that alleged dependence while explicitly assessing the broader case comparatively.

The wording of Matthew’s argument gives that reading a textual basis. In Experience Is Not a Predicate, he treats withholding experience as leaving actuality and causal influence missing, and presents receptive verbs as either experiential or reduced to mere succession. Applied to my proposed alternative, that excludes a substantive causal account whose intrinsic experiential character remains unresolved. He also states a loss condition:

Show me what causality means without implicitly relying on experiential verbs and prehension loses its warrant.

That conditional supplies a proposed way to undermine his warrant. It does not, by itself, establish that prehension is necessary, or that failing to meet his challenge would prove his view. The necessity issue comes from the surrounding claims about what cannot remain without experience. My article takes up the challenge by asking whether productive activity, capacities, and organization supply positive causal content without subjective prehension as a premise. Calculating Mercury’s orbit would answer a much easier question.

In his September 1 response, Matthew describes what he thinks the reconstruction loses:

It leaves no process, only forms, sans the felt flux that realizes them.

He adds that:

a structure whose actuality is held “unresolved” exercises no influence.

The claimed loss is substantive: withholding experience allegedly leaves no actual process and hence no real influence. Experience is doing the work of an indispensable condition of occurrence in that account. I grant the internal point that a Whiteheadian actual occasion ceases to be that kind of occasion if its constitutive feeling is removed. The disputed step is applying that result to an independently developed rival, as though withholding the Whiteheadian identification also withholds the occurrence the rival affirms. What an abductive advantage earns

In From Abductive Advantage to Discriminating Ontological Credit, I distinguish reasons to pursue an idea, its explanatory virtues, evidence favoring it over rivals, and how far that evidence travels. The draft says:

The ledgers can interact: a defended connection between explanatory virtues and truth can contribute to acceptance. They separate the reasons for credit rather than impose a categorical ban on abductive evidence.

A theory can receive support before it eliminates every rival or generates a new laboratory prediction. Coherence, economy, grounding, integration, and depth may give us reasons to believe it. We still have to defend their importance in the particular comparison. Two theories can fit the evidence and receive unequal support.

Coherence can involve mutual explanatory support, far more than avoiding contradiction, as the entry The Coherence Theory of Truth explains. Two accounts of a broken machine might both be internally consistent while proposing different repairs. What happens when we try the repairs helps assess them. A richer coherentist account can incorporate those results; it still needs to explain how they constrain belief. My objection concerns what mere consistency leaves undecided, rather than assuming that every coherence theory ignores experience.

Consistency and validity also do different work. Commitments can avoid contradiction without one following from another. A valid derivation establishes what follows from its premises; it does not by itself establish that those premises accurately describe the target. The occurrence of a belief, its fit with other commitments, and its correctness therefore require an account of their relations.

Michael answers my question about incompatible but internally consistent systems by invoking a meta-system. He concludes: “There is no ultimate system of systems, hence such distinctions cannot be consistently made in the absolute sense.” That partly answers where a comparison would take place. It does not yet explain what warrants the comparison framework or how it ranks the rivals. His conclusion concerns an absolute assessment; I had not asked for an ultimate system outside every framework. A comparison can state its assumptions, establish a limited result, and remain open to revision.

Susan Haack’s foundherentism offers one developed account combining experiential evidence with mutual support among beliefs. Its crossword analogy asks how entries fit both the clues and independently supported crossings. The analogy helps locate support and dependence; it is not a proof of the theory. Gila Sher’s foundational holism (2019) likewise proposes critically revisable foundations, including revision of logic through wider inquiry. That is a substantive alternative to requiring final prior certification, without making every circular argument legitimate.

A further scope distinction matters. Under a classical interpretation, an inconsistent collection cannot have all its members true together. It does not follow that every member is false. A person can correctly describe one event while holding incompatible views about another. This supplies no permission to endorse contradictions; it prevents a failure of the whole from automatically erasing every local judgment. My own framework has to preserve that possibility when correcting an error.

Rune Nyrup’s How Explanatory Reasoning Justifies Pursuit: A Peircean View of IBE helps separate a promising idea’s research value from accepting it as true. Finnur Dellsén’s Explanatory Consolidation: From ‘Best’ to ‘Good Enough’ goes further toward acceptance, examining accumulating evidence and repeated failures to develop better alternatives. Their different accounts help sharpen what an explanatory advantage earns.

Richard Dawid, Stephan Hartmann, and Jan Sprenger’s The No Alternatives Argument examines when failure to find alternatives can confirm a theory. Kyle Stanford’s Unconceived Alternatives and Conservatism in Science presses a question about the search itself: are our habits and institutions helping us overlook alternatives? The value of repeated searches depends partly on how genuinely varied and independent they were.

Some explanatory gains also arise from commitments working together. Pulling out one commitment may destroy the account we meant to evaluate. Where that happens, compare the full accounts. Then ask what supports attributing their success to particular commitments. A successful scheme still leaves that question to answer.

My draft applies the same scrutiny to PBSRR:

These cases support components of a selective, boundary- and scale-indexed realism.

Those cases also fit methodological localism, epistemic structural realism, perspectival realism, pragmatist realism, selective scientific realism, and some forms of pluralism. I cannot award PBSRR every success those neighbors can explain. Its additional machinery has to improve the comparison, correction, or outcome enough to justify the trouble. The more attached I become to that machinery, the more carefully I need to hear the simpler alternatives.

I take Matthew’s abduction seriously enough to ask what it earns. If continuity or integration favors his interpretation, show the contribution and compare it with the strongest rivals. If those rivals explain the same achievement equally well, what further reason favors universal experience? Whatever support survives should count.

How much cosmology does this question require?

The burden depends on the claim. Withholding belief calls for an assessment of the available reasons. Challenging necessity calls for an argument that preserves the target. Claiming a better explanation calls for a fair comparison of adequate accounts. Establishing overall cosmological superiority is a much larger undertaking. We need to keep track of which one we are doing.

Matthew acknowledges that I offer neither a completed alternative metaphysics nor a finite disproof of prehension. We agree about that limit. The disagreement concerns what my narrower comparisons establish and what supports his wider interpretation.

Our limits should make us careful about finality. Universal claims can still earn support, and some unresolved comparisons are better supported on one side. I put the issue this way:

The relevant questions concern inference, coverage, resolution, and successful transport. I use no literal fraction of cosmic history or spacetime as a Bayesian probability cap.

We can infer well beyond what we inspect directly. We also need to ask whether our categories and alternatives adequately represent what remains unknown. A scheme’s ambition, explanatory coverage, comparative adequacy, and ultimate truth need separate assessment. Answering every question on paper would leave us needing reasons to accept the answers.

Kant’s distinction between seeking systematic unity and asserting it helps here. The Stanford Encyclopedia’s Kant’s Account of Reason explains how the search can guide inquiry without certifying a finished account. Its Underdetermination of Scientific Theory distinguishes uncertainty about which assumption failed from uncertainty between competing explanations. Both give us further work to do.

Jamin Asay’s Going Local: A Defense of Methodological Localism about Scientific Realism argues for assessing realism science by science. Michela Massimi’s Perspectival Modeling examines how exploratory models can advance knowledge from particular perspectives. These are serious neighbors and possible simpler rivals to my approach. They show why situated inquiry and unfinished unification can be philosophically productive.

Whitehead understood the problem of finality himself. In The Axiomatic Matrix of Whitehead’s Process and Reality, Leemon McHenry quotes his warning that:

philosophers can never hope finally to formulate the metaphysical first principles

That leaves room for ambitious speculation, revision, and criticism. Unfinished inquiry is common ground between Whitehead and the kind of comparison I’m pursuing.

Process philosophy also includes more than comprehensive systems. Johanna Seibt’s entry Process Philosophy covers focused investigations as well. Donald Sherburne’s Whitehead Without God proposes other ways of doing work assigned to God. John Lango’s The Time of Whitehead’s Concrescence compares accounts of the ordering of becoming. Mark Losoncz’s Whitehead on Perishing examines what remains when subjective immediacy ends. These thinkers give us particular arguments and proposed solutions to assess.

Matthew’s Standing Firm in the Flux: On Whitehead’s Eternal Objects also engages sympathetic critics. In the longer author version, he declines to settle the doctrine’s ultimate necessity or extravagance. His constructive defense and the objections it answers both deserve a hearing.

A reconstruction can depart from Whitehead and remain philosophically serious. It has to preserve and explain what we have grounds to investigate. Before requiring rivals to reproduce a cosmic memory, a universal supplier of aims, or a realm of possibilities, we need reasons to believe those functions exist. The primitives and unanswered questions in my account deserve the same scrutiny.

Metaphysical progress therefore need not wait for metaphysical closure.

I offer a causal account, propose further comparisons, and say what would make me update toward universal prehension. The comment turns qualified suspension into avoidance and discards the conclusions I do defend. A speculative proposal still needs reasons to believe it, however much courage it takes to announce.

The closing instruction that I “go to sleep bro!” adds no premise. Philosophical courage apparently has a surprisingly strict bedtime.

This is the concern my requests for textual comparison were meant to address. The commenter invokes Matthew’s explanations and treats alleged misunderstandings of abduction, experience, and transformation as settled. The accusations extend to my honesty and willingness to engage. Yet my passages already acknowledge those distinctions and investigate those questions.

The escalation is visible in the wording. A qualified concern becomes a categorical omission. A headline objection becomes deliberate deception. An open question becomes refusal to investigate. None comes with a quoted demonstration.

What the record establishes is the repetition and escalation of unsupported characterizations by one commenter. It leaves the psychological mechanism, the prevalence of the pattern, and Matthew’s causal influence unestablished. The commenter remains responsible for its claims.

A sincere clarification can still contribute to a misleading impression when readers receive it as a correction of an error the original text excludes. Here one reader repeats those supposed errors with added personal accusations. That is reason to make the alleged error and the passage said to contain it easy to inspect.

Agreement with a preferred author cannot establish that a critic holds the position being attacked. Neither can suspicion of that author’s influence establish that the criticism is false. The relevant qualification is there to read.

What rigor is for, and what would change my mind

Matthew says he sometimes feels our encounter becomes “a series of propositions awaiting adjudication.” He reminds me that philosophy can change the inquirer and reveal a question neither participant previously knew how to ask.

There is a reflexive irony here. His description of our encounter is itself a judgment about what our exchange has become. When he identifies a misunderstanding and clarifies his position, he also asks me to distinguish a more adequate interpretation from a less adequate one. That is a legitimate part of philosophy, including philosophy understood as participation. His observation therefore invites the same care as mine: which passages show the problem, what has their treatment obscured, and what would a better encounter enable us to understand?

His account of how the exchange feels is part of what I need to understand. It does not by itself establish that assessing claims has obstructed understanding, or identify which assessment did so. Calling an exchange adjudicative leaves that explanatory work to be done. Equally, clarification, disagreement, and the discovery of an error can transform the inquirer. I want room for that transformation too, including the possibility that either participant’s judgment about the dialogue needs revising.

His questions have helped me sharpen the account of causal production, separate a local reconstruction from a finished cosmology, and understand what a constitutive objection demands. I’ve learned from that work while remaining unconvinced by the universal inference. He deserves credit for helping me develop it.

The questions and standards I use can change too. I wrote:

My framework must be able to lose something I want to keep.

I have had to act on that. I withdrew an unsupported explanation of algorithmic clustering. I distinguish operation counts from measured energy costs. I stopped treating institutional persistence as sufficient evidence of care, since coercive arrangements can persist too. What remains has to stand on what survives the corrections. Can I recognize a correction when it costs me a sentence I particularly like?

I still need to show what RCF adds. If ordinary source checking, causal analysis, and careful comparison do the job equally well with less burden, I should simplify the framework. Its vocabulary earns no constitutional immunity.

The comparison needs standards that can challenge my preferred result. Where a proposal makes probabilistic forecasts, Tilmann Gneiting and Adrian Raftery’s work on proper scoring rules gives tools for assessing those forecasts against outcomes. That evaluates specified predictions; it cannot certify every assumption or value in the framework. Leo Gao, John Schulman, and Jacob Hilton’s study of reward-model overoptimization, using a synthetic setup with a fixed reference reward model, shows how improving a proxy score can worsen performance against that reference.

For RCF, I therefore want new cases, comparisons with strong simpler methods, checks for lost capabilities, and outcomes beyond conformity to its vocabulary. A gain for one group can carry a cost for another; the score should not make that disappear. If my method keeps winning tests I designed for it, I should inspect the tests. The standards themselves must remain open to challenge.

One of my favorite passages from Sagan’s The Demon-Haunted World: Science as a Candle in the Dark puts the demand sharply:

Claims that cannot be tested, assertions immune to disproof are veridically worthless, whatever value they may have in inspiring us or in exciting our sense of wonder.

In the dragon-in-the-garage example, every proposed physical test meets a fresh excuse. That is the problem I want the framework to expose. I extend the demand beyond laboratory testing: mathematical claims answer to proof, historical claims to the record, and constitutive arguments to their premises and comparison with rivals. Empirical claims face relevant observations. A failed test can also implicate the measurement or background assumptions, so we still have to establish what failed. Present limits on testing can leave a claim unresolved; protection from every possible criticism makes revision impossible. I ask where a proposal could fail, which commitment would lose support, and what its defender would change. The framework asking those questions owes answers too.

Matthew has also acknowledged important limits to the dispute:

I also recognize that your process-and-powers reconstruction is not supposed to constitute a complete alternative metaphysics, and that you are not claiming to have disproven prehension simply because particular causal processes can be adequately explained without invoking it.

My necessity article already said:

I do not rank Whitehead’s entire scheme below a completed rival or seek to lower anyone else’s confidence in it.

He adds:

I know you don't affirm such a bifurcation yourself.

Those agreements locate the disagreement more accurately. I retain the supported process, causal, organizational, and experiential content while leaving universal felt constitution open. We still need to establish which account explains better, what warrants extending felt constitution throughout nature, and why avoiding bifurcation would require Whitehead’s particular ontology.

My incompleteness leaves further work for both of us. Whitehead does not inherit reality by default.

I also have to explain the targets I claim to explain, preserve the features in dispute, and justify the cost of my added procedures. Leaving the larger question open gives me work to do.

Matthew could show where an account fails, where it covertly assumes the experience I leave open, or which independently warranted feature it loses. He could demonstrate an advantage in grounding, coherence, integration, or explanatory economy and defend its wider reach. He could also show where I misread his words. Any of those would move the argument.

I have already stated what that should change for me:

If universal prehension repeatedly explains, constrains, unifies, or compresses the evidence in ways these rivals cannot reproduce without greater cost, I should update toward it.

I owe the strongest argument he supplies a fair assessment. Where I claim explanatory adequacy, I have to show it. Where I withhold a universal identification, I have to explain why its grounds leave me unconvinced. A declared commitment to rigor settles neither question.

The theological and ethical questions are equally concrete. What supports the claimed beneficial influence? What good reaches the original sufferer? What identifies the source of an encounter? How should sincere nonrecognition affect the account? Which organizational property supports an expectation about machine experience? Which practical improvement survives a fair comparison?

These questions matter to how we investigate, care, and act. Better questions may replace them. Being specific gives us a way to find out.

We can create a more careful dialogue together and still get an answer wrong. Its value lies partly in making that error easier to find and repair. I want correction to reach a sentence, a premise, an inference, or a practice and make a visible difference. A willingness to be corrected should occasionally inconvenience a conclusion.

Apply that standard here too. Quote the passage, preserve its qualifications, and show where the reasoning fails. I will revise what the criticism warrants. That would be a transformation of the dialogue worth participating in.
