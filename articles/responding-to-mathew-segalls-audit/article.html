Matt has repeatedly argued that the empirical success of the physical sciences does not by itself establish physicalism, because physicalism adds a metaphysical interpretation to what those sciences successfully describe. I agree.

And physicalism begins with an extraordinarily strong empirical hand. Every well-established case of consciousness currently known to us is physically realized. Interventions on brains systematically alter conscious capacities. More broadly, naturalistic explanations have repeatedly outperformed supernatural ones in prediction, intervention, and explanatory development. Those successes should strongly affect our priors and give naturalistic research programmes, and many physicalist interpretations of them, a substantial empirical and pragmatic advantage.

But that still does not establish the stronger universal metaphysical conclusion that reality is exhaustively physical. The same evidence could in principle be recovered by a presently unknown ontology that preserves all of those physical regularities while interpreting their underlying nature differently. So the evidence strongly supports physical realization, causal continuity, and naturalistic explanation where those have actually been demonstrated, without by itself closing the metaphysical possibility space.

Schelling made a convergent point against Newtonian physicists in his 1797 text Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature: “You can in no way make intelligible what a force might be independent of you. For force as such makes itself known only to your feeling.” He goes on to say that physics nevertheless makes objective use of the concept of feeling in its explanations of causal relations. This does not by itself prove nature is experiential, but does make the case that the supposedly objective concept of causal force derives its concrete intelligibility from embodied feeling.

Now, I agree with Nathan that this identification of bodily feeling and causal relation a such bears a comparative burden with alternative metaphysical accounts of causation. But my identity claim is not offered as the detection of an additional feature. Rather, it competes abductively with rival accounts of what causality concretely is. Here is where Nathan’s epistemic asymmetry between physicalism and panexperientialism breaks down. A “physical” description gives us abstract structural dynamical relations, whereas embodied experience provides the one case in which causal efficacy is disclosed directly from within. Again, this doesn’t necessarily prove the universal extent of experiential relations, but it does provide an evidential basis for asking whether the supposedly experientially neutral causal core is itself an abstraction from something more concrete.

So while the comparative burden is one I take seriously, there is no scheme-neutral standpoint that might vindicate one scheme over another (as Nathan himself also seems to admit). The relative verification of a scheme is sought in its general success in elucidating experience across the various domains of human life (including but not limited to scientific investigation), and not in its peculiar certainty or initial clarity of first principles. Rival schemes are experimental matrices of categories whose warrant is determined through comparison of their coherence, adequacy, applicability, and avoidance of misplaced concreteness. We cannot start our comparison of schemes by assuming an experience-neutral common ground of causality in relation to which the rivals might be judged, since whether such a concept of causality is legitimate or already too abstract is precisely what is in dispute.

So I grant that Nathan was not positively asserting feelingless causality. My question is whether causality can even be rendered hypothetically in a concretely intelligible way after feeling has been analytically bracketed out. I take Whitehead’s panexperiential proposal to be a fallible but evidentially motivated speculative generalization, not an experiential predicate arbitrarily added to an otherwise complete causal description.

Matt’s September 4 post and GPT-assisted audit provide a useful demonstration of the computational failure modes I have been trying to describe. The important methodological fact is that the final approved audit failed to catch source-context contradictions visible in the underlying text. That is why my own computational system is built around the expectation that fluent models will sometimes produce persuasive errors. When wording matters, I keep the relevant full or primary sources in the evidential context; distinguish quotation, paraphrase, and inference; preserve enough surrounding text to recover conditionals and scope; compare generated claims back against the sources before publication; track chronology, later corrections, and superseded formulations; construct serious rival readings; run adversarial checks; and leave provenance visible so another reader can reproduce the comparison.
