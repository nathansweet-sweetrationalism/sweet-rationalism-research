Three Conversations, One Methodological Crux

What Whiteheadian prehension, Martinus’s living cosmos, and a systems biologist’s challenge are teaching me about how ambitious claims earn the right to say more about reality

Over the last week or so I have been moving between three public conversations that began in different places. The process philosopher Matthew David Segall and I have been debating about Whiteheadian prehension, feeling, valuation, and panexperientialism. Karsten Jensen of New World Culture has been discussing Martinus with me, along with mystical experience, musement, the Absolute, literal truth, and spiritual science. Jason Hoskins, a systems biologist and cancer-genomics researcher who has also been involved in the Whitehead discussion, has been helping me stress-test how explicitly my method handles decomposition and rival reconstruction.

Here’s Jason’s most recent comment in the thread that began when Matthew restacked his response to me on his Substack, tagged me, and replied to an earlier comment of mine.

@Sweet Rationalism (Nathan), I enjoyed seeing our exchange incorporated into your impressively well-thought-out article, and I appreciate how it demonstrates the sort of progress you see your method capable of instigating within metaphysical debates. But two small clarifications about how you've characterized my position may be worth making.

First, when I said you'd convinced me that we were closer methodologically than my earlier "somewhere between" phrasing suggested, what changed was primarily my understanding of your methodology rather than my own. My commitment to holistic, abductive, recursively corrigible evaluation of metaphysical schemes substantially predates this exchange. As an example of my ongoing commitment to recursively apply my analogy warning to Whitehead’s scheme, I have been pressing @Matthew David Segall and others on the adequacy of Whitehead’s framing of potentiality in terms of eternal objects (e.g., https://substack.com/@jasonhoskins/note/c-290899540?r=1559h0&utm_source=notes-share-action&utm_medium=web). As an aside, I am working on a draft of a paper exploring that more deeply while also proposing some broader meta-philosophical considerations that I suspect would be of interest to you in your methodological endeavors. I’m not ready to fully tip my hand on the core of that paper (which I see as my “official” entry into the philosophical ring where I lay out the foundation of my larger philosophical project), but I’d love to get your careful eyes on it once I have a draft together, if you’re interested. Anyways, what I believe our conversation did was clear away some differences in vocabulary and argumentative style that had initially obscured how much overlap was already present in our approaches. And I think we’ve continued making reciprocal progress in that regard. For example, your clarification that the bridge is not an entrance exam but an accounting system applied after competing metaphysical experiments are developed addresses much of my "epistemology-first" concern.

Second, I think you slightly misread my comment about valuation. I wasn't saying your rival assumes reality is intrinsically non-valuational. I was identifying a conditional explanatory debt: IF genuine intrinsic valuation first appears at some organizational scale, then the account owes us some story about how valuational becoming emerges from organization that, at the more primitive scale, lacks valuation. Remaining agnostic about whether primitive reality is valuational is perfectly legitimate, but it leaves that question open rather than resolving it.

I also suspect this exposes one remaining methodological difference between us that I'm finding increasingly interesting. Your C + P factorization and ablation strategy is extremely useful, but I worry that conceptual separability doesn't always entail metaphysical separability. Sometimes removing P may change what C amounts to rather than simply yielding the same C minus an unnecessary predicate. That's closely related to why I've been thinking so much about analogy and conceptual topology. Our analytical distinctions are indispensable, but they can tempt us to treat potentially mutually implicative aspects of a process as independently constituted components. So, I'd want the recursive audit applied to the ablation operation itself as well.

Jason Hoskins, comment on Substack, August 25, 2026

We may be making some progress : )

As far as I can tell I am not revising my position on prehension. I’m expressing what I’ve been arguing for over a decade, including in Crossing the Threshold (2023). By “non-phenomenal” I mean non-presentational, non-representational, non-conscious, and non-intentional. I do not mean non-experiential in Whitehead’s technical sense. In CtT I describe how reflective consciousness might emerge from non-conscious prehensive feelings, and causal efficacy as the proto-subjective capacity to inherit the affective influences of objects. Causal efficacy is thus experiential in a way that runs far deeper than reflective cognition or conscious representation.

Feeling is neither bare causal responsiveness with an “experience” label pasted on it nor a mini-phenomenal quale added to an otherwise complete description of causal transmission. An actual occasion does not first exist as a subject and then have experience. The subject itself arises through the integration of the objects it inherits. In Whitehead’s terms, the many become one, producing a novel perspective and realization of value, which then perishes into superjective availability for subsequent occasions. The combination problem is only a problem for constitutive micro-psychism, which bears little to no resemblance to Whitehead’s process-relational ontology.

Whitehead’s use of analogy is proportional. I am not predicating feeling univocally of me and an elementary particle, as if the latter is enjoying what amounts to a dimmer version of conscious sense perception. Consciousness, intentionality, sense perception, etc., are all very special forms of experience arising in association with highly organized animal bodies. What is generalized is the more generic pattern of affective inheritance and self-unification, instantiated in radically diverse ways at different organizational grades of actualization. We must be careful to avoid projecting what is special in our human consciousness onto what may be generic in nature.

The radical empiricist premise is that our own embodied existence gives us a region of nature in which causal process is disclosed via intuitive participation in its concrete becoming, rather than as a formal structure applying externally. Our bodily experience is thus “our closest experience of the interplay of actualities in nature,” according to Whitehead’s scheme.

Much modern philosophy and science treats experience (the empirical domain) as though its primitive data were bare sensory qualia (patches of color, tones of sound, etc), with causal connection and physical order added subsequently by way of habit, conceptual synthesis, transcendental construction, or Bayesian world-modeling. Whitehead’s descendental method (as I refer to it) inverts this modern habit. As James also argued, our experience already includes its own interconnections. Causal efficacy is not inferred from otherwise neutral sensory appearances but is felt bodily prior to reflective consciousness.

I would suggest that your epistemology-first framing is itself metaphysically loaded. Before we can ask what we can know about reality, some conception of knowledge, experience, reality, evidence, and their proper relation is already operative. Epistemology is not some neutral tribunal that might somehow precede or preclude metaphysical background assumptions. Asking what our experience warrants us in saying about a reality supposedly hidden behind it already presupposes a particular picture of what experience is and how knower and known are related. Whitehead inverts the modern Cartesian-Kantian frame by beginning further down, not from an already constituted subject anxious about how its representations might reflect an independent world but from the objective vector-feelings (prehensions) that flow into each actual occasion.

So I do understand your distinction between our epistemic contact with reality and the intrinsic nature of causal reality, but I want to question its primordiality. Epistemic contact is always already an event occurring in the world. Before there is any representation of a world, before there is any intentional consciousness, there is the world causally entering into and helping to constitute an occasion of experience. On this account, there is no externally completed causal structure with an inaccessible qualitative property hidden behind it. An actual occasion’s “interiority” (a misleading term) is not hidden behind its prehensive relations, as though a substantial subject “has” experience of separate objects. An occasion’s internal constitution is its relational becoming: how an entity becomes constitutes what it is, to paraphrase Whitehead.

Nothing proves or exempts prehension/feeling from criticism. But there is no metaphysically neutral epistemological starting point from which criticism can commence. So all must be thrown into recursive scrutiny. You want an epistemic procedure that would license analogical passage from experience to reality. I will respond by asking what metaphysics of experience and reality has already been presupposed for them to appear as two independently constituted domains in need of a bridge?

I had planned to answer Jason in the comments and leave it there. Then I started putting his latest reply beside what I had actually written, sentence by sentence, mostly because I wanted to make sure I was not answering a disagreement he had not made. That changed the project. A communication problem had become part of the philosophical problem itself.

I also realized the exchange had grown beyond something only Jason and I would care about. Many of the people who read me are already interested in consciousness, metaphysics, religion, philosophy of science, and the harder question underneath all of them: how do we tell when a compelling interpretation has actually earned a claim about reality? Giving the conversation a little background seemed more useful than dropping a technical reply into the middle of a comment thread and expecting everyone else to reconstruct how we got there.

Jason said our exchange had “clear[ed] away some differences in vocabulary and argumentative style that had initially obscured how much overlap was already present in our approaches,” and that we had continued making “reciprocal progress.” I recognized the same pattern while drafting my answer. Once I put his exact wording beside mine, several apparent disagreements became smaller. Some survived. Others turned out to depend on how one of us was using a term, where we placed the inferential burden, or which level of the argument we thought the other person was addressing.

That made me go back through the Matt and Karsten threads with the same question in mind. I had already thought I understood where those disagreements were. Reading them again after Jason’s clarification made some of the old boundaries look less stable. A point I had filed under “substantive disagreement” sometimes turned out to contain a vocabulary problem. A point I thought had been settled sometimes reopened once a later clarification changed what the earlier claim was doing.

That changed how I wanted to present the exchange. I began quoting both sides rather than relying on retrospective summaries. In the reply below I say that “our wording crossed here more than our positions did,” that one phrase had made us “look farther apart than we were,” and later that I was “not yet sure we have found an incompatibility rather than a difference of emphasis.” Those are substantive diagnostics. Before solving a disagreement, I want to know whether the disagreement survives a careful reconstruction of what each person actually said.

Small vocabulary shifts matter a great deal in this territory. Feeling, valuation, experience, absolute, literal, constitutive, and separable can each carry several jobs. A conversation can drift while everyone remains convinced they are discussing the same proposition. Quoting the exchange makes that drift easier to catch, including when I am the one producing it.

I also noticed something else, although I did not trust it immediately. The same pressure point seemed to keep reappearing under different metaphysical nouns. At first I wondered whether I was simply doing the very human thing of seeing a pattern because I had started looking for one. Call it pattern-seeking, or apophenia in the stronger version: unrelated things can start looking unified once a favored lens is in hand. So I tried to break the comparison rather than admire it.

When a description, experience, analogy, model, or conceptual scheme already explains some shared body of evidence, what warrants adding a stronger ontological commitment to it, and what would have to happen for us to remove or revise that addition without throwing away everything that still works?

I now care more about that transition than about beginning with familiar camps such as materialism, idealism, mysticism, reductionism, or holism. The labels can wait. First I want to see what extra claim has entered, how far the evidence really travels, and what would happen if that extra claim lost.

I went back through the comments and then through the literature I had been using on constitutive explanation, decomposition, rival models, and underdetermination. The resemblance survived because it was more specific than “these people all talk about metaphysics.” In each case there was a relatively thin description that several rivals could share, a thicker interpretation being asked to travel farther, and a question about what evidence belonged specifically to that added commitment. Work on scientific constitutive abduction, context-sensitive decomposition, and countermapping and perspective-dependent reification gave me additional reasons to take that structure seriously while also warning me not to assume that my own decomposition was neutral.

Matthew: what does primitive feeling add?

My exchange with Matt has gradually narrowed, and one of the interesting things is that each clarification has removed one disagreement while making another easier to see. I originally spent time making sure the issue was not consciousness, representation, intentionality, or a Cartesian subject hidden behind causal relations. Once Matt explicitly removed those from primitive prehension, I thought one source of confusion had been cleared away. Then I realized that the clarification had made the harder question sharper: after all of those familiar phenomenal and cognitive features are stripped out, what positive content is left in feeling, and which parts of that remainder are supposed to travel with causal becoming generally?

A process-relational rival can accept causal inheritance and relational becoming. It can also allow integration, embodiment, novelty, and emergent organization without taking primitive becoming to be intrinsically experiential. The live question is therefore about the remainder: what licenses the further transport of affectivity, proto-subjectivity, perspective, and value into causal becoming generally?

The stakes are ontological, not merely terminological. Matt describes causal efficacy as something felt bodily prior to reflective consciousness, speaks of objective “vector-feelings” flowing into actual occasions, and says the generic pattern being extended across nature is affective inheritance and self-unification. In the related eternal-objects discussion he also treats each concrescence as a self-creating subject-superject and God’s primordial valuation as the unconditioned ground of relevance. Those claims bear directly on what causal becoming, agency, value, and possibility are in reality. My question is how much of that richer description the evidence and comparative explanatory work actually require.

There is an important correction to make here, including to my own way of describing the contrast. Matt is not missing corrigibility in the way a protected cosmology would be. He has explicitly said that prehension and feeling are open to criticism, that everything goes into recursive scrutiny, that discord with science can lower confidence, that a stronger rival could require amendment or abandonment, and that “the world can say no to our categories.” What remains less explicit is the local accounting: if primitive affect or proto-subjectivity loses while causal inheritance and relational becoming survive, which Whiteheadian commitments change, what stays, and how far does the failure propagate? That is why I keep asking for an update condition at the level of the disputed addition rather than treating willingness to revise the whole programme as enough.

Matt is free to run the full metaphysical experiment. Build the richer Whiteheadian model and see what it can do. My bridge question arrives when rival models are on the table. If another account preserves the successes we care about while withholding primitive affect or valuation, where does the Whiteheadian addition earn its keep? It might earn it through explanation, prediction, transfer, causal leverage, or some other advantage we can identify. The comparison has to tell us.

That shift mattered to me because it changed the kind of answer I was looking for. I was no longer asking Matt to defend the word experience in the abstract. I wanted to know which inferential work was being done by affective inheritance, proto-subjectivity, perspective, or value after the shared process-relational structure had already been granted. The question got smaller, but it also became harder to answer by changing vocabulary.

What is primitive experience, positively, and why should we believe that this added feature is actually there?

Karsten: when does interpretation become ontology?

The nouns change in my exchange with Karsten, yet the pressure point remains recognizable. His articleWhat is unconditional or absolute? began by accepting my correction that the original question had assumed an Absolute before establishing one. When he embraced infinitism and later said scientists and philosophers might not need to answer the Absolute question after all, I initially thought the main disagreement had largely resolved. Then his next essays made me reconsider what, exactly, had been resolved. The explicit demand for an Absolute had softened, but many of the same reality-bearing commitments were now appearing through a different register: Martinus’s living beings extending without upper or lower bound, facticity becoming absolute or unconditional, every encounter ultimately described as an encounter with the Absolute or God, and later a contrast between supposedly impoverished literal truth and richer mythopoetic truth.

I answered that essay in Musement Is Welcome. The Absolute Still Needs a Ticket.. My objection was never to musement, mystical experience, analogy, or speculative metaphysics. I was asking what licenses each promotion in the sequence: from relation to universal life, from lived nonduality to ontological nonduality, from facticity to unconditionality, and from there to God. That article is the bridge between the Karsten discussion and the Matt/Jason exchange because it asks the same comparative question under different metaphysical vocabulary: what does the thicker interpretation add, what survives without it, and what could make the addition lose?

How Karsten’s position constructively moved during the exchange

Karsten’s next response, A note about absolute certainty, moved us closer than I first appreciated. He says he is “happy to grant” nearly all of my objections, accepts Martinus as the producer of a cosmological myth, distinguishes his experience of immortality from an argument that he is literally immortal, allows the Martinussian reading to stand as an interpretation of that experience, and describes his own engagement primarily as inspiration rather than missionary conviction. Most tellingly, he returns to the claim that scientists and philosophers ought to address the Absolute and says: “Maybe they don’t. At least I’m not certain any more and I’m fine with that.” Reading that beside his first essay made me realize that a meaningful part of the framework had already moved while much of what he valued remained intact.

A Note on the Concept of Literal Truth moved the discussion again. Karsten says, “I’m not sure I ever took anything in Martinus’ worldview to be literally true.” My first reaction was that this dissolved much of the literal ontological dispute. On rereading the rest of the essay, though, I saw that the claims had changed type unevenly. Some had clearly become mythopoetic, existential, ethical, interpretive, or phenomenological. Others were still doing ordinary work in shared reality.

That matters because the same article also makes claims that are plainly factual or causal: that the modern scientific picture is a “giant, dead, meaningless machine” myth; that scientific literalism contributes to exploitation, animal brutality, and ecological harm; that Martinus was pointing toward an “animate reality”; and that his mythopoiesis may be no less true than the literal truths of science, “perhaps even on the contrary.” Karsten’s proposed loss conditions are mainly whether the worldview remains inhabitable for him and whether he discovers internal incoherence. Those are meaningful tests of existential fit and coherence. They do not yet tell us whether Earth is a living subject, whether plants are sentient, whether a scientific worldview caused a social harm, or whether one cosmology describes shared reality more accurately than another.

The deeper problem is that this criticism risks treating one historically influential metaphysical interpretation of science as though it were science itself. Science does not require the universe to be dead, meaningless, fundamentally mechanical, reducible to one privileged scale, or exhaustively captured by current models. Contemporary science routinely uses mechanistic, dynamical, probabilistic, ecological, network, effective, and multiscale models side by side. What those models owe is narrower: when they make claims about shared reality, they must remain vulnerable to evidence that distinguishes better from worse descriptions. Karsten’s own causal claims about animal exploitation and ecological harm depend on that same standard, because they compete with explanations involving economics, institutions, technology, consumer behavior, religion, and other factors.

My own provisional, boundary- and scale-relative ontology is a concrete counterexample to the picture he is criticizing. It is scientifically constrained while explicitly withholding the assumptions that reality must be dead, meaningless, reducible to one privileged scale, or intrinsically whatever our best current models happen to represent. Its rule is almost the reverse: “Do not add what the evidence did not earn. Do not subtract what the evidence did not defeat.” Stronger universal, intrinsic, identity, and necessity claims carry additional evidential burdens.

That is also why “scientific certainty” is an odd phrase within my framework. Science gives us degrees of warrant, successful predictions, interventions, robustness, transfer, and rivals that fail. It can make a claim extraordinarily well supported without converting it into something incapable of revision. Literal factual assertion and fallibility are perfectly compatible. What matters is whether reality still has some way to make us less confident when we are wrong.

There is another reason Karsten’s response surprised me. In Musement Is Welcome. The Absolute Still Needs a Ticket., the very article he was answering, I had already argued against almost exactly the position his next essay seems to place on my side of the table. I used my exchange with Massimo Pigliucci to distinguish methodological naturalism, physical realization, and physicalist completeness. Pigliucci had classified an earlier organizational account of mine as a sophisticated form of physicalism. I appreciated the judgment that the proposal deserved serious consideration while resisting the implication that scientific naturalism automatically commits us to the stronger thesis that matter and energy exhaust reality, that all higher-level properties reduce to lower-level physical facts, or that “physical” already names the intrinsic nature of everything that exists.

I also raised the familiar problem with defining the physical as “whatever fundamental physics says.” Current physics is incomplete, while ideal future physics risks making the category too indeterminate to settle the metaphysical question in advance. My conclusion was therefore not anti-scientific and not anti-physicalist. Physicalism may turn out to be an output of inquiry if the evidence earns it. I simply do not treat it as an input constraint. Higher-scale organization, emergent causal structure, effective patterns, intrinsic-nature agnosticism, and the possibility that our present ontological categories are incomplete all remain open.

So the disagreement cannot fairly be framed as Martinus’s living, meaningful cosmos versus my commitment to a dead physical machine. I had already rejected that dichotomy in the article Karsten was responding to. The remaining question is narrower: once either of us makes a claim about what shared reality is actually like, what distinguishes that claim from serious alternatives, and what would make us revise it?

That is where my upcoming article, The Musement Park Needs Safety Rails, picks up the thread. I think two questions have been sliding together: Is this claim meant literally? and How certain are we that it is true? A literal claim can be modest, probabilistic, and revisable. A myth can be profound, morally useful, psychologically revealing, or existentially transformative without functioning as a factual model.

The difficulty begins when success in one role is used as warrant in another. If a myth, spiritual interpretation, or phenomenological experience is used to tell us that plants are sentient, planets are subjects, modern science caused a particular social harm, or reality itself is fundamentally animate, then it has entered factual territory. The relevant question becomes: what would distinguish this account from a serious alternative, and what would make us conclude that this particular claim was wrong? That is the boundary I am trying to keep visible.

After reconstruction, our frameworks are much closer

Jason’s warning about comparing reconstructed wholes helps here. It would be a mistake to take Karsten’s earlier Martinussian picture, delete the literal propositions one by one, and call whatever remains “Karsten minus ontology.” His later replies reorganize the role those ideas play. Reconstructed on their own terms, they leave a recognizable whole: musement as a generator of possibilities; myth and metaphor as interpretive resources; contemplative experience as first-person evidence; existential inhabitability as a criterion for personal engagement; moral orientation as a practical aim; internal coherence as a minimum constraint; open criticism; and explicit willingness to revise at least some earlier formulations.

Once separated that way, a surprising amount of Karsten’s current framework fits mine. My methodology also gives imagination and abduction room to work before demanding evidential promotion. It does not send poetry, ethics, phenomenology, mathematics, history, and metaphysics before one laboratory tribunal. It asks what kind of claim is being made and then matches the error-control procedure to that claim. Karsten’s own movement from “philosophers and scientists ought to answer what is Absolute” to “maybe they don’t,” from apparent immortality to a report about how an experience has shaped his life, and from Martinus as cosmology toward Martinus as mythopoetic and existential resource already performs something very close to that decomposition.

Seen as a whole, Karsten and I may now agree on much more than our language suggests. We both leave room for musement, lived experience, interpretation, criticism, and revision. The remaining question is simply this: when those things are used to make claims about shared reality, what justifies that extra step? That is where existential or mythopoetic success becomes a question of public ontological warrant.

The deepest remaining difference with Karsten’s present framework is the correction machinery. He and Martinus welcome criticism; what I still cannot find is a rule set that gives criticism authority to lower confidence in a specific reality claim while preserving the rest. My framework, what I call Recursive Corrigibility Framework, makes that local revision explicit through rivals, loss conditions, independent checks, and failure propagation. Had that machinery been present from the start, it likely would have separated literalness from certainty, existential fit from external accuracy, and mythic resonance from ontological warrant much earlier. Karsten’s recent revisions are beginning to do exactly that informally.

Where Jason’s criticism helped me make a failure condition more explicit

Jason’s latest clarification matters because he locates less methodological distance between us than he first thought. He says his commitment to holistic, abductive, recursively corrigible evaluation predates our exchange. What changed, in his account, was mainly his understanding of my methodology. Once some differences in vocabulary and argumentative style were cleared away, he explicitly agreed with a principle I had formulated: speculative analogy cannot warrant its own generalization, imported categories remain open to serious rival reconstructions, and the thinner rival has to face the same scrutiny.

The difference I can presently see is mostly one of operationalization. My framework tries to turn those commitments into explicit local bookkeeping: claim types, rival-specific loss conditions, target-preservation checks, and rules for how failure propagates. Jason’s criticism gives me a more explicit local diagnostic inside that machinery.

Jason’s own comments also keep the metaphysical stakes visible. In the valuation exchange he asks whether richer organismic valuation is an elaboration of a more primitive vectorial character of becoming, and he entertains intrinsic directedness or valence within causal becoming as something substantive enough to compare against a scale-relative account. In his work on Whitehead’s eternal objects he asks whether their definiteness can really be separated from their position in a relational ordering, and he is exploring whether Whitehead’s relational insights can be extended into potentiality without treating eternal objects as pre-definite forms. I take those as live ontological alternatives under investigation. They are useful precisely because the method has to compare them without deciding their metaphysical status in advance.

His remaining concern lands inside a condition I had already stated. A thinner rival still has to preserve the explanatory successes of the richer view; a lower assumption count by itself earns very little. Jason pointed to a concrete way that condition can fail: removing P may alter what C itself amounts to. If the alteration destroys the relevant successes, the rival has failed the preservation condition.

That observation gives me a clean diagnostic. In my shorthand, an attempted operation that looks like C + P → remove P → C may actually produce C′. The comparison then has to ask what changed. Identity may have shifted. So may the organization, boundary conditions, causal profile, measured capacities, or explanatory role. Any target-relevant change can invalidate the claim that we merely removed an optional predicate.

Jason’s formulation then prompted me to apply that existing preservation requirement more explicitly to the Karsten exchange. If I simply removed the literal claims from Karsten’s earlier Martinussian picture and treated the residue as the same framework minus ontology, I would be committing exactly the kind of bad subtraction Jason was warning about. Karsten’s later replies had changed the role of the remaining pieces. Myth, experience, ethical orientation, and personal inhabitability were no longer functioning in quite the same way once literal assent was withdrawn. The right comparison was between the earlier and later reconstructed wholes.

I have therefore made target preservation explicit in the procedure. An ablation earns evidential force only after the alleged common core survives the operation well enough to remain the same target for the purpose of the comparison. Failed subtraction sends us to a different comparison, often reconstructed whole against reconstructed whole.

The audit also runs backward into the original theory. A scheme can build P so deeply into its definition of C that every rival loses before evidence enters. That would make theory-internal inseparability look like a discovered feature of reality. The paired diagnostic now checks both directions: whether subtraction manufactured independence and whether the starting conceptual topology manufactured inseparability.

The same pressure point keeps returning

By this point I had enough examples to worry about overfitting. Maybe I had built a method that could redescribe every disagreement as the same disagreement. That would be useless. So I started asking what would make the comparison fail. Matt’s question concerns the intrinsic character of causal becoming; Karsten’s mixes phenomenology, myth, ethics, and public ontology; Jason is largely questioning the architecture of comparison itself. Those differences are real. The common denominator only survives at a more abstract level: in each case I am trying to track when a successful description has earned promotion into a stronger ontological commitment, how far evidence gathered at one boundary or scale can travel, and whether the comparison still preserves the target it claims to compare.

The universal quantifier is where this becomes especially obvious. All becoming is experiential. All scales contain living beings. All reality is physical. All reality is nondual. Each claim travels farther than the local evidence from which it is usually motivated. I want the same burden applied in every direction: specify the transport, show what survives it, and identify a result that would stop it.

Parsimony becomes relevant only after the thinner account has preserved the target. A subtraction that destroys relevant explanatory structure has lost the comparison it was supposed to make. The richer theory faces a different risk: it can define the disputed category so deeply into the shared core that rivals lose by construction. There is also a quieter endpoint. Operationalization may reveal no remaining difference that prediction, intervention, counterfactual analysis, or explanatory comparison can currently reach. The framework can then record equivalence or underdetermination and stop carrying the verbal dispute as if evidence had chosen a side.

What I am building the method for

The recurring problem is one I keep running into from different directions: how do I give speculative inquiry enough room to discover something genuinely new without quietly turning the freedom to generate a possibility into permission to believe it? I have spent years dissatisfied with both extremes. If the entrance requirements are too strict, interesting metaphysics gets strangled before it can develop. If the exit conditions are too weak, every coherent worldview becomes capable of surviving indefinitely by redescribing whatever happens.

What I need from the methodology is an error-correcting constitution for inquiry. It should let musement, analogy, phenomenology, mathematics, abduction, metaphysics, and model-building produce candidate explanations without demanding premature proof. Afterward, those candidates have to meet serious rivals on terms that allow either side to lose something.

The conversations with Matt, Karsten, and Jason are useful to me because they stress different parts of the machinery. Matt forced me to distinguish general willingness to revise from a local update rule for a disputed ontological addition. Karsten showed how much of a worldview can survive when its claims are retyped from literal cosmology into phenomenological, ethical, or mythopoetic roles, while also exposing what happens when those roles drift back into public claims about reality. Jason already shares much of the recursive, rival-sensitive architecture and has been testing whether my own implementation preserves the target it claims to compare. None of those lessons requires the other person to adopt my framework. The framework is being developed partly by finding where my descriptions of their positions, and my own procedures, fail.

The procedure is less linear in practice than a checklist makes it look. A rival may force a construct to be redefined. A failed bridge can send the inquiry back to its boundary assumptions. New measurements can reveal that two variables I separated were coupled, while an intervention may expose a distinction that looked merely verbal. Claim typing, bridge construction, rival generation, severe testing, and failure propagation recur as needed. The path often loops backward.

Several failure modes motivate that recursion. Premature reduction can strip away a richer category simply because a thinner vocabulary looks cleaner. Metaphysical immunity lets a favored system absorb every result without paying an evidential cost. There is also the quieter failure of preserving a dispute after its rival formulations have become empirically or operationally equivalent. A useful method should be able to detect each of those without deciding in advance which ontology deserves to win.

A strong claim becomes more interesting after surviving that process. It has faced reconstructed rivals, boundary changes, independent evidence, and real opportunities to lose. If a large worldview contains both genuine insight and unsupported promotion, I want a method that can separate them well enough to keep what survived.

This is the problem underneath all three conversations: how can reality correct our conceptual schemes when every contact with reality is already mediated by some conceptual scheme?

Where the exchange has reached

Where I have ended up is not where I expected these threads to converge. Matt’s side has narrowed to the positive content of primitive feeling and the warrant for generalizing it. Karsten’s has narrowed in another direction: once his Martinussian commitments are reconstructed around mythopoetic, existential, ethical, and phenomenological use rather than assumed literal ontology, much of the methodological distance between us disappears. Jason’s comments then make me ask whether I am representing either of those shifts fairly unless I compare the reconstructed wholes rather than subtracting disputed predicates from an earlier formulation.

Jason’s latest comment loops the audit back through my own procedure. My original C + P comparison already conditioned a thinner rival’s success on preserving the relevant target. His objection makes the failure case easier to see: an ablation can be misapplied when that preservation requirement remains too implicit. The correction is now operational. Audit the ablation, check whether the target survived, and move to rival reconstructions when simple subtraction no longer describes what happened.

That is where the reply below begins. Jason gave me a concise way to expose an existing failure condition to direct inspection, which is exactly the kind of criticism I want the framework to retain.

That caution matters because the empirical and philosophical literature still gives us no license to treat differential regulation, organizational normativity, evaluative or action-selection value, phenomenally or hedonically felt valence, and intrinsic affective mattering as interchangeable. I also want to widen what I mean by “independent warrant.” Your abductive emphasis, together with the newer literature, gives me reason to avoid making that phrase sound interventionist. Aizawa and Headley’s “Scientific constitutive abduction” documents cases in which scientists infer constitutive structure abductively from explanatory fit plus substantial background knowledge, including cases where the posited entities are not directly detected. Those cases count against an intervention-only tribunal. The authors also leave open whether scientists thereby genuinely confirm those constitutive hypotheses. My takeaway is to broaden the admissible routes of warrant while keeping explanatory attractiveness from certifying itself.

The 2025–2026 literature on inference to the best explanation is divided enough that I would rather expose the conditions of the comparison than legislate one universal rule for abduction. Woodward, “The place of explanation in scientific inquiry: Inference to the best explanation vs inference to the only explanation”, rejects IBE as a general form of truth-directed inference.

Roche and Sober, “Inference to the Best Explanation, Bayesianism, and the Screening-Off Challenge: A Critical Review”, show how explanatory virtues can be screened off by evidence already doing the confirmatory work. Dellsén, “Inferring to the Best Explanation from Uncertain Evidence”, shows how uncertainty about evidence can reverse which explanation is inferable as best. Mohammadian, “Theoretical Virtues, Truth, and the Epistemic Aim of Scientific Theorizing”, offers contemporary counterpressure by defending theoretical virtues as an epistemic aim of scientific theorizing.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry “Abduction” likewise treats abduction’s normative status as disputed. For me, the practical question becomes local: which virtues are independently constrained, what evidence supports them, which rivals were actually considered, and does the ranking survive uncertainty about the evidence?

Would you agree that the holistic abductive comparison must itself remain corrigible? My bridge tribunal becomes broader than interventionism, while abductive comparison remains answerable to evidence quality, unconceived rivals, and the possibility that the criterion of “best” was partly produced by the conceptual topology being evaluated.

Two philosophy-of-science papers put useful pressure on opposite failure modes. They call it illicit reification when a feature “belongs exclusively to a scientific perspective” but is treated as part of the target (Walsh & Rupik 2023).

Neither paper settles our metaphysical case. They do, however, support a discipline I suspect we both want:

Do not infer metaphysical independence merely from successful analytical decomposition.

Do not infer metaphysical inseparability merely from theory-internal mutual implication.

Try rival decompositions and rival reconstructions, then ask which dependency relations survive the change in representation.
