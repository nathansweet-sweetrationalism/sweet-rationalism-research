The Process Philosopher, Matthew David Segall, recently published Experience Is Not a Predicate, partly in response to my essay I Wanted Erik Svensson’s Argument for Physicalism to Be Stronger. So I Tried to Build One Myself..

The first problem is source fidelity: the question Matt presents as my “straightforward question” is not a quotation of the question I actually asked. It is his paraphrase, removed from an explicitly two-branch IF / may comparison in which I deliberately left primitive intrinsic character open. In that move, the logical form changes from conditional comparison to positive commitment, my epistemic stance changes from suspension of judgment to primitive nonexperience, my ordinary comparative use of experiential predicate is recast through Whitehead’s theory-internal meaning of predication, the rival changes from a strong process-relational alternative into a “nonexperiential” and then “wholly feelingless” one that I would likewise reject; and the burden of proof shifts by assigning me an abrupt-emergence problem my actual argument never incurred.

That transformation matters because much of what follows in the rebuttal depends on it.

His response consequently makes it look as though we disagree about detachable experiential properties, primitive nonexperience, bare succession, intrinsic valuelessness, abrupt emergence, and whether later feeling would require explanation.

I never defended those positions.

Nearly every major proposition he presents as an objection was already a premise of my critique or an explicit exclusion I had stated to him before he wrote the article.

Much of the rebuttal therefore answers arguments we already agree are inadequate, while the one question on which we actually diverge remains unanswered on its own terms.

My original question was deliberately conditional. I asked, in effect: IF what Matt calls primitive feeling contains no more than causal uptake, integration, transition, and response, then a non-panexperiential process ontology may recover most of that explanatory work without universally characterizing primitive causal actuality as experiential; IF, instead, primitive feeling includes some irreducible affective or subjective character, then that additional difference becomes substantive and needs warrant.

The important words were IF and may. The first branch was not my ontology. The two branches were the comparison.

That is why changing suspension of judgment into positive nonexperience changes more than the wording. It changes which hypothesis I am defending, which rival Matt needs to defeat, what explanatory debts belong to me, and what would count as answering my actual question. Evidence against a positively “wholly feelingless” ontology does not by itself discriminate universal primitive experientiality from a rival that accepts the same causal inheritance, receptivity, integration, novelty, organization, and real influence while leaving primitive intrinsic character open.

I want to be clear about why this matters to me on a human level, because I am not trying to win a debate with Matt. I genuinely value these exchanges precisely because I want other people to find the places where my map is wrong, where I have smuggled in an assumption, or where an inference I thought was careful does not survive scrutiny. If Matt thinks my words entail a position stronger than the one I believe I stated, I sincerely want him to show me the bridge so I can examine it and revise accordingly.

What is difficult is being asked to defend positions I have repeatedly disavowed, especially when my actual words are available to quote. I would much rather he quote me directly, show how the disputed conclusion follows from what I wrote, or simply ask me whether that is what I mean before building an argument around it. That is not a request for gentler criticism. It is a request for criticism that reaches me.

For avoidance of doubt, I am not claiming that experience is a detachable property of otherwise complete substances; not assuming a Cartesian subject separated from reality; not claiming that physics establishes primitive nonexperience; not identifying prehension with consciousness, representation, intentionality, or presentational phenomenology; not demanding a single laboratory falsifier or a metaphysically neutral tribunal; not reducing causation to Humean succession or mathematical description; not asserting that primitive nature is intrinsically valueless; not assuming that experience must appear at a sharp evolutionary threshold; not claiming a thinner rival wins by simplicity alone; and not denying that a demonstrated transition from nonexperience to experience would require explanation.

Those exclusions were already in the public exchange before Matt’s article was published. The disagreement that remains after restoring them is much narrower: what warrants the final move from the process-relational structure we substantially share to the universal claim that primitive causal actuality itself is intrinsically experiential, rather than leaving that intrinsic character open?

The actual shared ground is extensive.

Matt and I both reject a Cartesian subject standing outside nature, dead external mechanism, Humean empty succession, naive subtraction of an experiential property from an otherwise unchanged substance, and an unexplained jump in which feeling simply appears from nowhere. We both treat relational becoming, causal inheritance, embodied world-contact, integration, novelty, organization, and the constitution of subjects through relations as serious explanatory targets. We both distinguish primitive prehension from reflective consciousness, representation, intentionality, and presentational phenomenology. We both allow familiar animal consciousness to depend on highly organized living bodies, and we both treat metaphysical schemes as corrigible rather than exempt from criticism. Once those agreements are held fixed, the apparent many-sided dispute contracts to one live question: does causal actuality as such warrant a universal experiential, affective, proto-subjective, perspectival, or valuational characterization, or should primitive intrinsic character remain open until something discriminates that interpretation from its strongest process-relational rivals? I am suspending judgment on that residual. Matt promotes it into the ontology of actuality itself.

Matt’s positive case includes radical empiricism, but part of the pressure for that promotion comes from what he says happens to competing nonexperiential pictures: they collapse into bare succession, leave actuality blank, lose receptivity or valuation, or incur a brute emergence problem. Those failures would count against the models that actually make those commitments. They do not by themselves select universal primitive experientiality over the agnostic process-relational rival I stated. This is the same dependency rule I applied in my Erik Svensson essay. I criticized the move from the retraction of one purported panpsychist model to a much broader verdict on panpsychism. It would be the same inferential overreach to let the failure of a dead-mechanistic or positively nonexperiential rival promote the whole panexperiential universal while another rival preserves the shared causal structure without asserting primitive nonexperience. A local failure updates the commitments that generated it; it does not automatically promote every stronger commitment carried by the surviving theory.

In May, responding directly to Matt in The Physicalism-Panpsychism False Dichotomy and Organizational Closure as the Falsifiable Third Option, I had already named this same structure

“comparative underdetermination plus asymmetric burden placement.”

The rule was the same: evidence shared by serious rivals is non-differential,

“presupposition in the epistemological sense does not license attribution in the ontological sense,”

and

“showing that one picture is inadequate does not establish that the alternative is correct.”

The present exchange repeats that problem at finer resolution. Whitehead’s critique of vacuous mechanism, the internal coherence of his scheme, and the failure of positively nonexperiential models can all count against those targets while leaving the universal experiential characterization unearned relative to the process-relational rival that leaves primitive intrinsic character open.

I therefore want the remaining question in view before we get lost in Whiteheadian terminology, chronology, or the sociology of who is speaking to whom. Granting Matt’s process-relational ontology and every point of shared ground, what comparative reason warrants treating causal receptivity itself as intrinsically experiential, affective, proto-subjective, perspectival, or valuational rather than leaving primitive intrinsic character open?

Before any answer can count as a reply to that question, the question itself has to survive paraphrase.

Before the rebuttal begins, three things change

Matt introduces the section addressed to me by saying,

“He asks a straightforward question,”

and then gives this formulation:

“if causal connection can survive the removal of concepts like ‘feeling/prehension’ or ‘experience,’ why should anyone favor my interpretation over an apparently lower commitment agnosticism that keeps relational becoming while jettisoning what he calls the experiential predicate?”

That sentence is not a quotation from my essay. It is Matt’s reconstruction of it. The difference matters because the reconstruction changes the semantics, the logical form, and finally the rival being evaluated before the rebuttal begins.

The sentence in my physicalism essay responding to Evolving thoughts from Sweden’s article was explicitly conditional in both directions:

“IF primitive feeling contains no more than causal uptake, integration, transition, and response, then a non-panexperiential process ontology may recover most of the explanatory work without the experiential predicate. IF primitive feeling includes an irreducible affective or subjective character, the difference becomes substantive and needs warrant.”

The two branches were the test. Notice the words doing the logical work: IF, may, and the question that follows. IF marks each branch as conditional; may marks the first consequence as provisional. IF feeling contributes nothing beyond a causal-process description, the comparison asks what is gained by universalizing the experiential language. IF it contributes irreducible affective or subjective content, that positive residual becomes the object of inquiry: what is it, what work does it do, and what warrants carrying it beyond the cases in which experience is already known? The next move in the essay was the question:

“A rival can grant relational becoming, embodied world-contact, emergent subjects, integration, causal inheritance, and scale-sensitive organization while suspending judgment about universal affectivity. What observable, explanatory, phenomenological, or conceptual result should favor Matt’s interpretation over that lower-commitment rival?”

The leap in Matt’s paraphrase is from:

“IF primitive feeling contains only the shared causal-process features, a rival may preserve much of the work”

to:

“Nathan has removed experience and is defending a nonexperiential world.”

My use of predicate in that essay is fixed by its immediate context. I asked the physicalist,

“what evidence belongs specifically to the word ‘physical’ when it is used as an intrinsic metaphysical predicate?”

I then asked Matt the paired question:

“what evidence belongs specifically to the word ‘experiential’ when it is generalized to causal becoming as such?”

A few paragraphs later I summarized both as scope problems:

“Both moves transport a predicate outside the evidential region in which it was established.”

The comparison concerns the warranted scope of an attribution. Its grammar is logical and evidential: what licenses extending physical or experiential beyond the region in which the relevant evidence has established it? The argument requires no Whiteheadian theory of propositions, no detachable-property ontology, and no prior commitment about primitive intrinsic character. It requires only the ability to compare what rival accounts share with what one of them additionally claims.

Matt uses predicate in a specifically Whiteheadian way. Earlier in the same response, he says energy can function as a predicate because it is an abstract quantitative measure or determinable pattern of concrete happenings. In the section addressed to me, he explains that the patterns predicated of actual occasions are eternal objects, whereas experience is not such a pattern or optional quality because experience names concrete actualization itself. His own qualifier identifies the boundary:

“Within Whitehead’s scheme, ‘experiential’ cannot be construed as a predicate.”

He then states the constitutive identity more strongly:

“To be actual is to feel and be felt.”

Those claims can be internally coherent within Whitehead’s conceptual architecture while leaving the external comparison open.

A simple semantic substitution test makes the difference visible. Replace experiential predicate in my conditional with universal experiential characterization:

“IF primitive feeling contains no more than causal uptake, integration, transition, and response, then a non-panexperiential process ontology may recover most of the explanatory work without universally characterizing primitive causal actuality as experiential. IF primitive feeling includes an irreducible affective or subjective character, the difference becomes substantive and needs warrant.”

Nothing in the argument changes. The same premises remain conditional, may remains modal and provisional, the same two branches remain live, and the same comparative question follows. Universal experiential characterization, the claim that causal actuality is experiential, and my original evidential-scope use of experiential predicate occupy the same inferential role.

Now substitute the specifically Whiteheadian reading Matt’s rebuttal attributes to the phrase: an optional quality or predicable abstraction attached to an otherwise constituted actual occasion. The sentence no longer expresses the argument I wrote. It becomes a different question about subtracting the wrong kind of property from Whiteheadian actuality. The interpretation that preserves the inferential role of the surrounding argument is the logical and comparative use established by the text; the interpretation that breaks that role is supplied by the framework doing the reconstruction.

This is also a shift between argumentative levels. My sentence operates in a comparative metalanguage: one account universally characterizes causal actuality as experiential, another preserves much of the same process-relational structure while suspending judgment about primitive intrinsic character, and I ask what warrants preferring the stronger universal. Matt answers from inside one of the theories being compared: within Whitehead’s scheme, experience is not predicated of actuality because concrete actuality already is experiential. A theory-internal rule can determine what follows once that theory is adopted; it cannot by itself establish why the theory’s disputed universal should be adopted over a rival.

For the grammatical objection to invalidate the comparative question rather than merely report Whitehead’s internal vocabulary, substantial Whiteheadian premises have to be in place already. Enough of the scheme must be accepted for actual occasions to be the relevant units of concrete actuality, for experience to name concrete actualization itself, and for causal efficacy to be understood through prehensive feeling rather than as a structure whose primitive intrinsic character remains open. The later rebuttal adds further commitments when receptivity is pressed toward minimal interiority, selective integration toward aim and valuation, and the agnostic rival toward bare succession or brute emergence. These are substantive premises of the programme under comparison, not neutral grammatical facts.

Three evidential levels therefore have to remain separate. First is internal coherence: given Whitehead’s categories, does the identification of actuality with experience fit the rest of the system? It may. Second is internal explanatory integration: does that identification unify causal inheritance, novelty, valuation, embodied experience, and subject formation within the scheme? That can be a genuine theoretical virtue. Third is external comparative warrant: does the specifically experiential characterization explain, constrain, predict, transfer, or survive intervention better than serious rivals that preserve the shared causal-process structure while differing on primitive intrinsic character? Success at the first two levels does not automatically deliver the third. A framework can make a proposition necessary relative to its own premises without making those premises necessary relative to the evidence. Theory-internal necessity is not external evidential necessity.

That distinction also clarifies what Matt’s title can establish. Experience is not a predicate may accurately state an internal Whiteheadian relation between actuality and experience. My question is external and comparative: why should actuality be universally characterized in that Whiteheadian experiential way rather than left intrinsically open after the shared causal-process commitments have been granted? Both statements can be true at once. Whitehead’s technical semantics therefore cannot, by themselves, discharge the comparative burden.

The first transformation is a framework-relative semantic substitution across argumentative levels: a term functioning in the comparative metalanguage is retyped through the internal ontology of one theory being compared. Because that substitution does not preserve the inferential role of the source sentence, the resulting grammatical problem belongs to the reconstruction rather than to the original conditional. The second transformation is conditional promotion: one IF branch becomes the premise attributed to me while the second branch, the one that says irreducible affective or subjective content would itself need warrant, drops out. The third is rival substitution: suspension of judgment about primitive intrinsic character becomes jettisoning experience, then an agnostic nonexperiential account, then a wholly feelingless process from which feeling must erupt on a special date.

Matt’s technical meaning is not required anywhere for my argument to work; importing it is required for his grammatical rebuttal to have a target. The strongest Whiteheadian reply begins after that grammatical objection. It has to show why the premises that make causal actuality experiential should themselves be preferred to serious reconstructed rivals. Internal coherence, systematic integration, and explanatory elegance can contribute to that abductive case, but they are not equivalent to external confirmation. The comparative question remains: what evidence, explanatory constraint, intervention, phenomenological result, or successful transfer is better accounted for because primitive causal actuality is experiential rather than because the shared causal-process structure is present?

What the substitution does once readers encounter it first

The public setting makes accuracy about that distinction unusually consequential. Matt is currently Interim Chair and Associate Professor in Philosophy, Cosmology, and Consciousness at the California Institute of Integral Studies, and his Substack profile lists more than 5.7K subscribers. I am an uncredentialed, self-funded independent researcher doing this work in the limited time I can afford to give it. When Matt summarizes my position for an audience that has not followed several days of our comment exchanges, that summary is likely to become the argument many readers think I made. If it assigns me Cartesian separation, primitive nonexperience, dead mechanism, a detachable experiential property, or a sudden evolutionary switch after I had already denied those positions in his own threads that he’s responded to extensively, the effect is predictable: my actual question looks elementary, internally confused, and easy to dismantle before many readers ever encounter it in my words.

That asymmetry has a material opportunity cost. Every hour I spend reconstructing what I actually said, disentangling my position from arguments I never made, and correcting impressions already distributed to a much larger audience is an hour I cannot spend reading, testing, revising, or developing the research programme itself. Most people who encounter the original characterization will never read the correction. Some may be precisely the philosophers, scientists, researchers, and potential collaborators whose criticism, engagement, or institutional support could help turn work I now pursue in spare hours into a sustained programme. A public reconstruction can function as a reputational filter and collaboration bottleneck when it makes a rival look confused before readers encounter the rival in its own words.

That is especially consequential because I approached this exchange in almost the opposite spirit. I was trying to help identify the strongest surviving panexperiential claim, separate it from the process-relational commitments a serious rival can already grant, and build a research programme capable of surviving adversarial scrutiny. Collaboration of that kind requires each side to preserve the strongest version of the other’s position through the comparison. If one side can advance the positive programme while the other repeatedly has to recover its argument from stronger objections it had already excluded, the collaboration becomes structurally asymmetric and scarce research time is redirected from discovery into representational repair.

This is the failure mode I have been trying to make corrigible

I have encountered the same structural problem before. In documented exchanges around Michael Levin’s Platonic morphospace, Levin and David Resnik’s Mind Everywhere programme, and discussions involving Jacob Foster and the wider Platonic Space programme, I have repeatedly had to distinguish my actual third position from familiar rivals such as reductive materialism, scientism, or naive one-shot falsificationism. Private exchanges with Timothy Jackson forced the same distinction from another direction. My physicalism essay was itself built around refusing exactly this compression: physical realization is not exhaustive physicalism; process-relational agnosticism is not primitive nonexperience; demanding update conditions is not demanding that one failed experiment annihilate an entire metaphysics.

The recurring structure is narrower and more important: a position explicitly formulated to escape a familiar binary gets translated back into one side of that binary, and the reply then spends its force defeating the familiar position rather than the stated third option.

The pattern has a recognizable sequence.

First, a third position is stated. Second, it is translated into a familiar opponent: scientism, reductive physicalism, naive falsificationism, dead mechanism, or primitive nonexperience. Third, the response mobilizes strong arguments against that familiar opponent. Fourth, the success of those arguments is allowed to appear as pressure on the third position even though the inferential bridge from the third position to the familiar opponent was never established. The objections can be strong and still miss the dispute. Here they are strong against the wrong target.

That substitution can also shift the burden of scrutiny. If my request for an update condition is redescribed as

“one experiment must falsify the whole metaphysics,”

the conversation becomes a defense of metaphysics against naive Popperianism rather than an answer to what should make one commitment revise while the rest of the programme survives. If my process-and-constraint realism is redescribed as reductive physicalism or scientism, the discussion becomes a defense of lived experience, value, emergence, or irreducibility rather than a comparison with the process-relational naturalistic alternative I actually offered. If agnosticism about primitive intrinsic character becomes wholly feelingless process, the discussion becomes an emergence problem I never incurred.

Once the rival is replaced, the distinctive metaphysical addition can appear protected simply because the substituted critic is attacking something cruder.

This is why the request to quote me is not rhetorical bookkeeping. It is a provenance test. What exact sentence licenses the stronger position being rebutted? What inferential steps take us from that sentence to the attributed view? Which of those steps are mine, and which were supplied by the critic? If the attribution cannot survive that reconstruction, the downstream rebuttal has lost its target even if every premise of the rebuttal is otherwise sound. A corrigible debate should be able to answer those questions before building several pages of consequences on top of the attribution.

And a metaphysical programme should not become harder to criticize merely because requests for discriminating evidence or revision conditions are repeatedly translated into cruder positions that the programme already knows how to defeat.

The reason this matters to my own work is larger than this exchange. I am trying to develop an ontology, epistemology, and ethos that can function as error-correcting constitutions for computational systems: rules for preserving serious rivals, distinguishing uncertainty from negation, tracing claims back to their sources, exposing inferential substitutions, and revising without quietly rewriting the problem.

If a reasoning system can turn a carefully delimited position into an easier one, preserve that substitution through summaries or memory, and then reason confidently against it, that is exactly the kind of failure I want those constitutions to catch before it scales.

The consequences I care about reach beyond academic conferences, book sales, personal income, or staying relevant in academia. Computational systems are rapidly gaining capability and reach; the 2026 Stanford AI Index reports 88% organizational adoption and generative-AI adoption reaching about 53% of the population within three years. When systems increasingly mediate information and decisions for real people at global scale, representational errors can propagate far beyond the conversation in which they began.

Clarity is preservation of inferential provenance

There is an unexpected recursion here back to the Erik Svensson essay that prompted my original article. Erik’s strongest methodological complaint was about obscurantist language and the muddled thinking it can conceal. I agreed with the demand for clarity while arguing that the demand has to be applied recursively. An argument can be obscured by subtraction or by addition. Simplification can erase distinctions until a difficult position becomes a familiar caricature; technical reconstruction can add framework-specific commitments until a comparatively ordinary question becomes a different metaphysical problem. Erik’s essay sometimes illustrates the first failure mode. Matt’s response illustrates the complementary one.

Clarity is preservation of inferential provenance, regardless of whether the language is simple or technical. We should be able to identify which meaning belonged to the source, which interpretation was introduced by the critic, which propositions were asserted, which were conditional, and which explanatory debts arise only after additional commitments have been supplied. A short sentence can hide a caricature; a sophisticated reconstruction can hide a substitution. The relevant test is whether the structure of the disagreement survives the transformation.

That test is especially important here because the language becomes more philosophically elaborate while the hypothesis space becomes less faithful to the source. My paired use of physical and experiential asked about evidential transport. Matt’s Whiteheadian reconstruction adds a substance-quality problem to that question. My argument then presents two conditional possibilities for what primitive feeling might contain. His paraphrase retains one branch. My rival suspends judgment about primitive intrinsic character. His reply progressively redescribes it as nonexperiential and finally as wholly feelingless. Each step adds structure, and each addition must be marked as interpretation rather than silently returned to the source as something I asserted.

Those transformations accumulate into a new hypothesis. The source position leaves primitive intrinsic character open while preserving real causal influence, relational becoming, organization, gradual development, and the later existence of conscious experience. The reconstructed position is positively nonexperiential, mechanistic, valueless at primitive scales, and burdened with a sudden emergence problem. Once that replacement has occurred, Matt can dismantle exactly the positions I would dismantle too. A reader who encounters his reconstruction before my words can therefore watch a sophisticated rebuttal succeed against bad arguments and reasonably infer that those bad arguments were mine. The sequence itself makes the live disagreement look easier than it is.

The central cost is epistemic. If two interlocutors actually agree that Cartesian substance dualism, dead external mechanism, Humean succession, abrupt miraculous emergence, and naive property subtraction are inadequate, then another demonstration of their inadequacy does not update the disagreement between those interlocutors. It creates an appearance of argumentative progress without moving the proposition actually under dispute. Criticism is useful only when the success or failure is credited to the hypothesis that generated it. Source fidelity is therefore part of evidential bookkeeping: before a rebuttal can count against a position, the position being rebutted has to be the one the source actually committed to.

Obscurity can enter through needless complexity, but it can also enter through false simplification. Clarity requires preserving the structure of the disagreement, not merely making one side easier to describe or easier to defeat. When that structure is lost, readers can receive a real argument against the wrong hypothesis and update their beliefs in the wrong direction. The result is an epistemic error: it directly degrades our collective ability to find out which proposition is actually better supported.

What Matt clarifies, and what that clarification actually establishes

Matt begins his reply generously, saying that I have for the most part represented his process-relational panexperientialism faithfully and distinguished it from easier forms of substance-based panpsychism. I appreciate that because preserving this distinction is essential to the comparison. Whitehead’s actual occasions are events of becoming, not little psychic marbles. Consciousness, in Matt’s account, is a very high-grade achievement of animal nervous systems rather than something every occasion possesses. What is supposed to be universal is a thinner experiential register tied to causal inheritance itself.

Matt’s response clarifies how predicate functions inside his Whiteheadian framework. To keep the comparative level unmistakable, I will use universal experiential characterization, experiential universal, or universal attribution of experientiality from here forward. The terminology changes; the argument does not. The comparison requires no Whiteheadian theory of predication and no detachable-property ontology. It asks whether the Whiteheadian identification of actuality with experience has comparative warrant over reconstructed rivals.

Whitehead’s internal grammar is clear on its own terms. Actual occasions do not first exist and then acquire experience. They are experients,

“drops of experience,”

whose being is their feeling. Experience names concrete actualization itself and, within Whitehead’s scheme, is not a predicable quality added to an otherwise complete occasion. That clarification tells us what the Whiteheadian identification entails once the scheme is adopted. It does not establish why the premises that generate that identification should be preferred to rivals that preserve the shared causal-process work while withholding primitive intrinsic character.

By the time Matt’s essay appeared, the simple

“Whitehead minus one detachable experiential property”

reading had already been ruled out in the exchange with Jason Hoskins and in my own replies to Matt. Jason’s criticism of my C-plus-P shorthand helped me formalize why: if removing P turns the alleged common core C into some materially different C-prime, simple ablation has failed. A rival then has to reconstruct the target under another conceptual organization and recover the relevant work before it earns any advantage. My current Recursive Corrigibility Framework now makes that preservation requirement explicit. The comparison is between reconstructed wholes, not a surgical deletion from Whiteheadian actuality.

The remaining comparison therefore operates one level above Whitehead’s internal theory of predication. One developed scheme says that concrete causal actualization is intrinsically experiential. Another developed scheme can accept relational becoming, real causal influence, state dependence, organization, integration, novelty, embodied interaction, and later conscious experience while leaving the primitive intrinsic question open. We can compare those reconstructed wholes without pretending that Whitehead’s internal categories are detachable pieces. What Matt clarifies is the internal architecture of his scheme. What remains to be established is its external comparative advantage on the disputed experiential universal.

Ben Snyder’s comment beneath Matt’s essay unexpectedly makes this boundary even sharper. Snyder is not an outside voice in the discussion. Matt has described him as a friend and fellow Whitehead enthusiast with whom he has explored interpretive disagreements for several years, has publicly discussed Snyder’s 2024 Process Studies paper on Whitehead’s propositions, and has credited Snyder for critical comments on his own scholarly work. Snyder agrees that predicate language is not the best formulation, but he recasts the issue through Whitehead’s theory of facts or states of affairs: the unity involved in exemplification is not supposed to be one more relation requiring another relation to bind it, but a concrete unity whose model is given in experience. What makes his comment especially useful here is what he says next. He explicitly notes that it remains controversial why experience should receive this foundational role, calls his reformulation only a precisification that opens the need for justification against other metaphysical theories, and adds that the causal and mereological structure of prehensions still needs clarification before the argument is strongly justified.

That is very close to the distinction I am trying to preserve: making the Whiteheadian proposal more exact improves the proposal, but greater exactness does not by itself decide the comparison.

Snyder’s Bradley-regress framing also gives us a more precise place to look for rivals. The regress is a genuine problem about what unifies properties, relations, or constituents into a fact rather than leaving us with another relation that itself seems to require relating. But the metaphysical literature has not converged on experience as the only possible answer. Philosophers have proposed non-relational ties, internal unification strategies, special nexus relations, and other accounts of how facts or states of affairs hang together. The Stanford Encyclopedia discussion of Bradley’s regress and its treatment of the unity problem for states of affairs make the plurality of proposed solutions explicit. So even if Whiteheadian experience offers a coherent response to the regress, the next question is comparative: why is experiential unity preferable to the strongest rival account of unity? Snyder’s comment, importantly, leaves that question open too.

There is a useful symmetry here with arguments in philosophy of religion. Kant’s critique of the ontological argument is often compressed into the slogan that existence is not a real predicate, but the broader lesson is more useful than the slogan. A conceptual relation can be necessary within a description without establishing that anything answering to the description is instantiated. The Stanford Encyclopedia’s discussion of Kant’s objection notes that even if existence is necessarily contained in a concept, that gives only a relation among concepts until actuality has been established independently. The useful mirror is structural: constitutive indispensability inside a conceptual architecture cannot by itself establish that the architecture is instantiated by the world.

Imagine a physicalist answering Matt in the same form.

“Physicality is not a predicate attached to actuality. Concrete actuality simply is physical actuality. Every real causal process is therefore physical. Remove physicality and nothing concrete remains.”

That may be coherent within a sufficiently strong physicalism. It still would not answer Matt if he asked why exhaustive physicalism should be preferred to a rival that accepts all established physical realization while withholding the universal completeness claim. The physicalist would have placed the disputed conclusion inside the account of actuality and then pointed to its theory-internal indispensability. Matt would be entitled to ask for more. I think he owes himself the same question.

A related distinction came up for me in a very different argument about whether there are logically valid arguments for God. Validity is a relation between premises and conclusion: if the premises are true, a valid deductive conclusion must follow. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy makes the elementary but important distinction that soundness additionally requires the premises actually to be true. Metaphysical comparison normally asks still more. Are the constructs well specified? Do the premises have independent warrant? Does the argument survive uncertainty in auxiliaries? Does the ontology add explanatory work over serious rivals? Does the claimed identity remain robust when we change representation? Internal coherence is a virtue, sometimes a very substantial one, but a metaphysics does not become a description of reality merely because reality would have to look that way if we first granted the metaphysics.

That point becomes sharper if Matt answers that experience is not an addition at all: causal actuality simply is experiential actuality. An identity claim does not escape the comparative burden by being stated as an identity. It changes the form of the claim; the world still has to warrant the identity. If experiential contributes nothing beyond causal uptake, inheritance, integration, transition, and response, then a rival may preserve all of the relevant structure while treating the experiential vocabulary as an optional interpretation; the disagreement becomes thinner and may approach a difference in description rather than a demonstrated extra fact about reality. If experiential contributes something more - affective inheritance, proto-subjectivity, intrinsic mattering, perspective, self-enjoyment, realization of value, or some other positive content - then that residual is exactly what requires comparative warrant. Making experientiality constitutive of actuality inside the Whiteheadian scheme locates the residual inside the scheme; it does not establish the residual independently.

This is also where the physicalist mirror becomes more than a rhetorical reversal. In my earlier physicalism essay, I tried to preserve the empirical carrier while stripping away claims the evidence did not yet earn. The physical realization of every well-supported case of animal consciousness, the effects of anesthesia, lesions, stimulation, pharmacology, development, injury, and the broader success of physically continuous explanation all give physical-organizational accounts a major evidential advantage over views requiring an independently acting nonphysical cause. They do not automatically establish universal nervous-system necessity, reductive identity, the absence of fundamental mentality, or an exhaustive physical ontology. The same rule now cuts in the other direction. Physical dependence does not by itself establish physical exhaustiveness; causal relationality does not by itself establish primitive experientiality. The standards are symmetric even though the evidence is not: the empirical support for physical realization and physical causal constraint is vastly stronger than the distinctive empirical support presently available for universal primitive feeling. That asymmetry should remain visible while both universal additions remain separately accountable.

The strongest reply I can reconstruct is constitutive, not grammatical

I want to reconstruct what seems to me to be Matt’s strongest underlying intuition here and give it the strongest case I can. The charitable reconstruction goes deeper than the word predicate: it seems to me that Matt is resisting my factorization itself. My comparison asks whether we can distinguish a shared process-relational carrier, including causal inheritance, receptivity, integration, transition, novelty, organization, and real influence, from the further claim that this actuality is intrinsically experiential or affective. Reading his own formulations together, the strongest answer I can reconstruct is that there is no such carrier independently of experiential actuality. Experience is

“coterminous with causal connection in nature”

actual occasions do not first exist and then acquire experience;

“To be actual is to feel and be felt.”

If that reconstruction is right, my C-plus-X decomposition has cut through what Matt takes to be a constitutive identity and the comparison has been badly drawn.

The grammatical objection tells us how Whitehead represents actuality after the Whiteheadian identification is granted; the constitutive argument has to tell us why that identification should survive comparison before it is granted.

That is the version of the argument I want to test, because it is much stronger than the claim that I chose the wrong noun in one question out of 10,000 words. It also seems to me that the positive bridge Matt is reaching for is stronger than a bare appeal to Whitehead’s definitions. I read his appeal to radical empiricism this way: in our own embodied existence, causal efficacy is not encountered merely as external succession but as felt inheritance, bodily reception, and the influence of the past in the present. On this reconstruction, the next move is to generalize that form of receptivity beyond the organism. When Matt presses words such as inheritance, uptake, and response toward minimal interiority, it seems to me that the intended force is that the experiential interpretation makes concrete causal influence intelligible in a way an abstract causal diagram does not.

This is genuine evidence for something important. At least some causal actuality is experientially disclosed from within. Matt’s warning that successful structural or mathematical description can leave concrete actuality underdescribed also belongs in the comparison. The remaining bridge runs from some causal process is experientially disclosed to causal process as such is intrinsically experiential. The evidence contains at least two features at once: causal-relational organization and experiential disclosure. The universalization has to tell us why the experiential side, rather than only the causal-relational pattern, is the invariant that travels.

This is where my earlier physicalism argument places unusually sharp pressure on the comparison. I gave physically grounded explanations a substantial evidential advantage because physical realization and physical intervention are not speculative additions. Every well-supported case of animal consciousness we can investigate is physically realized, and changes to the relevant physical organization systematically alter conscious capacities. Those successes establish a powerful empirical carrier while leaving the stronger identity claim, that reality is therefore exhaustively physical, separately exposed. Matt deserves exactly the same discipline in reverse. Our embodied causal existence really is experientially disclosed, giving his radical-empiricist bridge genuine evidential content. Universal primitive experientiality remains the further claim whose scope still has to be earned.

The evidential asymmetry therefore survives the mirror test. Physical realization and physical causal constraint have a large interventional record; exhaustive physical ontology does not inherit all of that support for free. Experiential disclosure is certain in paradigmatic conscious systems and may illuminate causal efficacy from within; universal primitive experientiality does not inherit all of that support for free either. The standards are symmetric even though the evidence is not. Each universalization must be paid for by evidence that reaches the disputed scope.

Structurally, this is why the factorization seems to me to be the pressure point in our exchange. If the physical and process-relational carrier can be held fixed while primitive experientiality is treated as a separable interpretive residual, then the carrier’s enormous empirical successes cannot be counted again as distinctive evidence for the residual. The strongest reading I can make of Matt’s response is that it tries to block that move by treating experientiality as what concrete causal actuality already is rather than as an added interpretation. The most important question is whether Matt can show that a serious reconstructed rival preserving real influence, inheritance, receptivity, integration, novelty, and later conscious experience is incoherent unless it makes the same experiential identification.

If he can show that, I should move substantially toward him. If every phenomenally neutral reconstruction of causal receptivity either collapses into Humean succession or secretly reintroduces the experiential content it denies, then the agnostic middle I have been defending would lose. But if interventionist, powers-based, dynamical, organizational, or other process-relational accounts can preserve genuine difference-making and receptivity while leaving primitive intrinsic character open, then Whitehead’s constitutive identity remains a coherent metaphysical interpretation rather than a conclusion forced by the common causal core. That is the argument I think we should now have.

Where the omitted third option changes the inference

The upstream paraphrase becomes logically consequential when Matt asks what fills the blank left after eliminating experience. His answer is that actuality itself disappears, and from there the supposed rival accumulates liabilities until it owes an account of how feeling emerges from a positively feelingless base. But the earlier reply he was answering said that the rival:

“need not assert fundamental nonexperience; it simply declines to universalize the disputed predicate until something discriminates against it.”

There are therefore three positions in play, not two: universal primitive experientiality; universal primitive nonexperience; and a process-relational account that remains agnostic about primitive intrinsic character. An argument against the second does not select the first while the third remains live. This is the point at which rewording changes the logic rather than merely the tone. Once agnosticism is rewritten as nonexperience, Matt can charge the rival with an emergence problem it did not yet incur; once causal realism is rewritten as abstraction, he can say causal influence has disappeared; once scale-relative valuation is rewritten as primitive valuelessness, later valuers look miraculous. The apparent absurdity is generated by commitments introduced in the retelling, and defeating those commitments cannot be counted as defeating the omitted third option.

That sequence would be forceful against a philosopher who had positively asserted a base layer of intrinsically nonexperiential matter and then declared the emergence of feeling somebody else’s problem. My position leaves that unresolved ontological slot open. My current boundary- and scale-relative realism is deliberately more committed about structures, processes, causal dependencies, and patterns that earn robust leverage than it is about universal claims concerning intrinsic nature.

“Not established”

and

“established false”

are different epistemic states, and collapsing them manufactures explanatory debts that the agnostic never incurred. Primitive intrinsic nature remains unresolved in my account. If I later argue that primitive reality is definitely devoid of experiential character, the emergence challenge becomes mine in a much stronger form. Until then, the live alternatives include primitive experientiality, primitive nonexperientiality with some form of emergence, and genuine underdetermination while science and philosophy improve the comparison.

There is a simple logical point here that I want to state without letting the notation disguise it. At the level of reality, perhaps primitive actuality either is experiential or it is not. At the level of warranted belief, however, there are at least three available attitudes: affirm primitive experientiality, affirm primitive nonexperience, or withhold judgment because the evidence has not yet selected between them. Evidence against confidently affirming primitive nonexperience does not automatically become evidence for confidently affirming primitive experience while the third attitude remains live. This is why attacking a positively feelingless ontology cannot, by itself, pay the evidential bill for panexperientialism.

The counterfactual makes the point clearer. Suppose every developed theory that positively characterized primitive reality as intrinsically nonexperiential were decisively defeated tomorrow. That would be important. It would lower confidence in those theories. It still would not, without an additional discriminating step, establish that causal actuality is intrinsically experiential. The remaining space could still include primitive experientiality, intrinsic-character agnosticism, neutral or dual-aspect reconstructions, and possibilities we have not formulated well enough yet. A rival’s loss transfers evidential credit only to the extent that the losing result was differentially expected and the serious remaining alternatives have actually been compared.

The symmetry also constrains me. If I move from

“affectivity and perspective may be scale-relative achievements”

to

“affectivity and perspective are definitely absent primitively and arise only at later scales,”

I have added a positive claim and acquired the corresponding burden. If I claim Whiteheadian panexperientialism is false rather than underdetermined, I owe evidence that selects against it. Suspension of judgment remains open to criticism while refusing to manufacture a positive ontology before the comparison has earned one.

There are at least three clear ways Matt could move the burden rather than merely redescribe it. He could show evidence or explanatory success that is appreciably more expected if primitive actuality is experiential than if the strongest agnostic process-relational rival is true. He could show that what I have treated as the shared causal core is actually incoherent or explanatorily impossible without the experiential content, rather than merely inseparable from it by Whiteheadian definition. Or he could show that the supposedly agnostic rival covertly commits itself to primitive nonexperience, or to some other positive metaphysics carrying the same unresolved liabilities. Any of those would materially change the comparison. Defeating a feelingless rival that I never asserted does not.

Matt’s programme is built from a positive metaphysics of process, causal efficacy, embodiment, inheritance, concrescence, and radical empiricism. A narrower step in the argument risks taking the structure of a gap inference: if a rival’s incomplete account of intrinsic causation or of the emergence of feeling is treated as positive evidence that primitive causal actuality was experiential all along, the reasoning moves from

“the rival has an open explanatory problem”

to

“therefore my positive ontology receives support.”

That inferential bridge requires independent justification.

Douglas Walton’s work on arguments from ignorance clarifies when absence is genuinely evidential. Negative evidence can be informative when a good search was likely to have found the expected thing. If a theory predicts a detectable signal and repeated, severe searches fail to find it, absence can become evidence. Lack of a currently satisfying explanation, by contrast, supplies positive support for a specific alternative only when the alternatives make different expectations. In this case the right question is comparative: what should we expect to observe, explain, or recover under panexperientialism that we should not expect under the strongest non-panexperiential process-relational rival? The difficulty of the problem alone cannot carry that burden.

Receptivity is where the disagreement has finally become interesting

I think that Matt’s strongest positive argument is not really about consciousness. It is about causal receptivity. Inheritance, uptake, and response all suggest that what came before makes a difference to what comes after. An occasion is not sealed off from its past. Matt asks us to press on the meaning of this reception until we recognize a:

“minimal interiority.”

If we refuse, he says, the verbs deflate into bare succession and causation itself disappears.

That is a much stronger and more precise challenge than the claim that matter cannot somehow produce mind. It may even point toward something important. The conceptual space is wider than experiential interiority versus Humean emptiness. Contemporary causal theory includes interventionist, counterfactual, structural, powers-based, mechanistic, and dynamical approaches intended to capture real difference-making rather than mere temporal sequence. Woodward’s interventionist framework, for example, represents causal claims in terms of how changes in one variable would alter another under suitable interventions; the Stanford Encyclopedia overview describes the account in exactly those difference-making terms. These approaches leave intrinsic nature open while showing that

“minimal interiority or empty succession”

is not an exhaustive map of the available conceptual space. A system can possess causal powers, dispositional sensitivities, historically conditioned dynamics, organizational closure, regulatory response, and intervention-sensitive dependencies without the description already deciding whether any of that has affective or subjective character. To defeat that middle position, Matt needs to show that causal efficacy itself cannot be adequately characterized without the extra experiential content.

The word receptivity therefore needs more care than either side may initially want to give it. A receptor can be receptive to a ligand. A control system can be sensitive to an input. A dynamical process can inherit a state. A causal variable can alter what downstream variables do. An animal can also feel what happens to it. Ordinary language lets those senses bleed into one another because familiar organisms often instantiate several of them at once. Whitehead’s project deliberately resists the bifurcation that would make causal nature utterly alien to experience, so I understand why Matt sees this continuity as philosophically important. But the continuity is the thing being proposed. If

“receptivity”

begins as the undeniable fact that prior conditions causally alter later states and ends as intrinsically felt reception, then the shift is carrying metaphysical weight. If minimal interiority means nothing richer than causal sensitivity itself, the claim becomes easier to universalize but less obviously experiential in the sense that motivates panexperientialism. Using one flexible verb across the sequence cannot substitute for showing which features remain invariant as we move from organismic feeling to primitive causal interaction.

The same problem recurs when Matt says valuation disappears. Valuation can name several different processes: differential causal response, regulation relative to viability conditions, selection relative to a goal state, outcomes that matter for maintaining an organization, learned reward, intrinsic attraction or aversion, affective valence, or phenomenally felt pleasure and pain. Jason Hoskins’s objection pushed me to separate these instead of allowing one word to ferry evidence between them, and contemporary consciousness research gives us independent reason to keep them apart. Walter Veit’s gradualist work on consciousness and valence, for example, treats valence and consciousness as questions requiring analysis rather than assuming every form of value assignment already carries phenomenality.

If Matt’s primitive valuation means only that an occasion selectively integrates inherited conditions relative to a trajectory or aim, a process-relational organizational rival may be able to reproduce much of the work without committing to affect.

If it means that outcomes intrinsically matter to the occasion in something resembling attraction, aversion, or felt significance, the claim becomes substantially richer, and that richer version is precisely where I want the evidence or comparative explanatory gain. A thin definition should not establish ubiquity and then quietly lend its universality to a thick definition.

His treatment of novelty creates a similar forced choice. Matt writes that removing experience, receptivity, and aim leaves only randomness constrained by law. Novelty has more live options than subjective aim or randomness. Nonlinear dynamics, developmental interaction, symmetry breaking, historical contingency, path dependence, evolutionary variation, niche construction, and multiscale constraints can all produce genuinely new configurations without first deciding whether every transition has an experiential aim. Matt can reply that these remain abstract descriptions of concrete becoming. That returns us to the same open question: what warrants interpreting the concrete remainder as experiential rather than leaving its intrinsic character unsettled?

A loss condition has finally appeared. Now it has to be allowed to lose.

One of the most useful sentences in Matt’s reply is his proposed loss condition:

“Show me what causality means without implicitly relying on experiential verbs and prehension loses its warrant.”

I have been asking for something in this neighborhood for months, so I want to credit the move before criticizing it. Matt is no longer merely saying that Whiteheadian prehension can be challenged in some global, unspecified sense. He identifies a result that would bear directly on the disputed feature: a genuinely causal account that does not smuggle experience back in.

The difficulty is deciding what could possibly count as success. Suppose I offer an interventionist model in which changing X changes Y under stable background conditions. Suppose another philosopher gives a powers account in which properties dispose systems toward characteristic manifestations. Suppose a dynamical account captures inherited state, asymmetric influence, counterfactual dependence, constraints, and real changes in what later states can become. Matt could accept that such accounts preserve genuine causal efficacy without experiential content, in which case his loss condition has teeth. He could also say that all of these are merely abstract because concrete sensitivity is already receptivity and receptivity, rightly understood, is experiential. If that answer remains available no matter how much causal work the rival preserves, the loss condition is only apparent.

Antony Flew’s old challenge in Theology and Falsification supplies a useful structural diagnostic here. Flew’s famous complaint concerned assertions that survive counterevidence through enough successive qualification that it becomes difficult to say what their negation would look like. His target was religious language, and his own falsificationism is too blunt to serve as a universal tribunal for metaphysics. The useful diagnostic survives: if a claim announces a failure condition, we should be able to describe a possible route by which the condition could actually be met. Otherwise the claim has inherited the rhetoric of exposure without the risk.

Matt has explicitly said elsewhere that nothing exempts prehension from criticism and that

“the world can say no to our categories.”

I take those statements seriously. His August 21 clarification was one reason I described his programme as having become more explicitly corrigible. The present essay gives us the opportunity to turn that principle into a local test. If a rival preserves real influence, inheritance, asymmetric dependence, integration, novelty, and later conscious experience, what nonverbal criterion would make Matt accept that the rival has captured causality while leaving minimal interiority unsupported? If every adequate causal relation counts as experiential by definition, the answer will always be no, and the theory will have protected the disputed term inside the admission rules for causality itself.

A related theological comparison sharpens the same structural risk. A necessary-being theology can be logically tight once necessity, explanatory dependence, causality, and divine attributes are loaded into the premises, yet that does not establish that the premises are instantiated. Likewise, a theology can become difficult to challenge if every apparent failure is reclassified as a misunderstanding of what divine love, action, or presence really means. Any sufficiently rich conceptual system can become semantically self-sealing if it retains unilateral control over what counts as a successful rival. Physicalism can do it. My own framework can do it. The argument risks special pleading if theory-internal identity is treated as sufficient warrant for experiential actuality while analogous identity claims from physicalism, idealism, or theology are rejected for lacking independent support. The rule has to cut symmetrically across all of them.

Gradualism is interesting, but it does not yet discriminate panexperientialism

Matt also offers a prospective empirical expectation. If process-relational panexperientialism is on the right track, he says, consciousness science should resist convergence on an arbitrary phylogenetic point where experience abruptly switches on, and gradualist frameworks should prove increasingly fruitful. This is better than retrospective accommodation because it says where he expects the science to move. I would count that as progress in the research programme.

The problem is contrast. Gradualism is not unique to panexperientialism. Mona-Marie Wandrey and Marta Halina’s recent review, The Evolution of Animal Consciousness, surveys substantial disagreement about the evolutionary distribution of consciousness while emphasizing that the dispute depends heavily on which indicators we trust. Veit explicitly develops a gradualist evolutionary account without requiring that experience be coterminous with causation.

A world in which phenomenal consciousness emerges through increasingly complex biological organization can therefore generate gradual transitions, fuzzy boundaries, and disagreement over thresholds without containing universal primitive prehension.

That makes Matt’s prediction a prediction of a broad family rather than of the distinctive Whiteheadian residual. If future research favors degrees over one sharp switch, several rivals win together.

To test universal prehension specifically, we need some expected difference between a world where causal actuality is minimally experiential everywhere and a matched world where consciousness emerges gradually from organization while primitive intrinsic nature remains nonexperiential or unknown.

Matt may reasonably answer that such a difference will not be a simple measurable magnitude because prehension is a constitutive metaphysical hypothesis. Fine. The comparison can use explanatory adequacy, coherence, transfer, constitutive reasoning, and other appropriate evidence. It still needs a result whose evidential direction is not shared by the strongest rival.

There is another complication. Matt distinguishes primitive experience from consciousness. If neuroscience eventually converged on a biologically meaningful transition associated with minimal phenomenal consciousness, he could say that the result identifies the emergence of consciousness rather than the emergence of prehension. That may be perfectly consistent with his ontology. The consequence is narrower: the proposed phylogenetic prediction has weak leverage on the universal prehension claim if contrary consciousness-level results can always be assigned to the emergence of consciousness rather than prehension. A research programme needs to specify which result would count against its distinctive commitment, not merely which results it expects to accommodate.

The historical vocabulary problem is still sitting there

Matt’s new essay makes his present position unusually clear: primitive experience is non-conscious, is not a predicate attached to substances, and names the concrete process of causal actualization. That clarity makes an older question more pressing rather than less. Across our earlier exchanges, Matt’s positive language for primitive experience has moved through several formulations that do not obviously have the same extension in contemporary philosophy of mind.

On December 17, 2024, in Why the World Is Unfinished, he described prehension as having

“an experiential quality”

and as being

“like qualia in that sense.”

On May 3, 2025, in Reality Is Process, he answered a direct question about Whiteheadian feeling with

“He means affect: the qualitative ‘what-it-is-like’ of experience.”

In January 2026, There’s No Scientific Evidence That… included language about what it is like, awareness, self-enjoyment at every level, and pervasive subjectivity. His August 21 clarification then specified that

“non-phenomenal”

means non-presentational, non-representational, non-conscious, and non-intentional rather than non-experiential in Whitehead’s technical sense. His 2019 paper Whitehead and Media Ecology had described causal efficacy as presubjective affective inheritance, while the recent exchange also used proto-subjective.

The historical issue concerns the stable positive content of the experiential term. In contemporary usage,

“qualitative what-it-is-like”

language usually points toward phenomenal character. Presubjective suggests a process prior to a constituted subject; proto-subjective sounds as though some rudimentary subjectivity is already being attributed. If Matt has one stable Whiteheadian concept underneath all of those expressions, I want the positive definition that makes the continuity clear. If the language has been narrowed or clarified through criticism, that is exactly the kind of revision a progressive research programme should record.

This history creates an evidential-slippage risk between thin and thick formulations. Matt’s most defensible universal claim can become extremely thin: every real causal process has receptivity, inheritance, or minimal interiority. Near the end of the essay, however, the language becomes richer. Nature is described as pervaded by

“experients”

and

“valuers,”

and the nonhuman world becomes

“intrinsically ensouled”

in a minimal sense. If the thin formulation establishes ubiquity while the richer formulation supplies the philosophical significance, the bridge between them has to be explicit. If the thin claim means little more than genuine causal sensitivity, the road to

“valuer”

and

“ensouled”

needs additional bridges. If the rich terms are already contained in the primitive notion, then the primitive notion carries more evidential weight than mere receptivity can establish by itself.

The ethical and ecological conclusions require a separate evidential ledger. Matt says that relating to nature as intrinsically ensouled can transform one’s experience and that the stakes of recognizing experients and valuers throughout nature are enormous. A metaphysical picture can reorganize attention, sympathy, behavior, and what people notice. That causal effect is evidence about what adopting the worldview does. It is a different evidential question from whether the worldview correctly describes the intrinsic nature of the entities involved. If transformative usefulness is allowed to confirm ontological truth, the reasoning risks an appeal to consequences. Transformative usefulness and ontological warrant therefore need separate ledgers.

Matt’s questions deserve direct answers, including the one I cannot fully answer

Matt’s clearest question to me is what fills the blank if experience is eliminated. My answer begins by restoring the actual premise: experience is established where the evidence supports it, while primitive intrinsic nature remains open. At the scales where evidence is strong, I am prepared to commit to real causal dependence, processes, constraints, organizations, effective patterns, and embodied systems; at the level of primitive intrinsic nature, I am prepared to leave more unresolved than Matt is. That is suspension of judgment, not a covert ontology of dead matter. If future evidence favors primitive experience, the framework should move toward it; if it favors a genuinely nonexperiential base, it should move that way instead; if the rivals remain indistinguishable, the honest result is underdetermination.

His harder challenge is what causality means if we strip away experiential verbs. I do not presently possess a final metaphysical account of what causality is in itself. Interventionist, counterfactual, structural, powers-based, and dynamical approaches tell us a great deal about causal dependence and difference-making without settling intrinsic nature. My framework mostly uses causation operationally: what variables change when other variables change, what interventions alter outcomes, what mechanisms transmit influence, what counterfactual structure survives, and how those relations transfer across contexts. Matt can reasonably say that this still leaves the question of concrete causal actuality open. That openness leaves the Whiteheadian answer as one live interpretation; it does not settle the question in Whiteheadian terms.

The emergence challenge attaches only if both sides of the transition are established. If genuine experiential or affective character occurs at one organizational scale and is absent from the relevant processes below it, then a serious account owes us a transition mechanism. This would not be enough:

“it just appears”

Matt’s image of a:

“special date”

therefore targets a positive nonexperience-plus-switch model, not the agnostic rival I described. Gradual organizational emergence remains one live possibility, universal primitive experience another, and suspension of judgment a third.

Matt’s challenge belongs in my own research programme too. If RCF or my provisional ontology eventually begins asserting what causal actuality intrinsically is, I will owe more than intervention maps and scale-relative causal models. Whitehead’s insistence that abstract causal description may leave concrete actuality untouched is a legitimate pressure on any ontology that becomes too comfortable equating predictive structure with exhaustive reality. My framework should preserve that pressure while leaving the intrinsic-metaphysical winner open to comparative evidence.

The questions his response still leaves open

After all the clarification, a smaller set of questions now carries most of the disagreement. The first is semantic and substantive. When Matt says genuine receptivity reveals:

“minimal interiority,”

what positively distinguishes that interiority from causal sensitivity, state dependence, dispositional response, or constraint-conditioned uptake? The second concerns claim type. Is:

“to be actual is to feel”

being offered as a definition internal to Whitehead’s system, an abductive hypothesis, a phenomenological generalization, a constitutive metaphysical claim, or some combination of these? Different claim types can receive different kinds of warrant, but we need to know which burden is being carried.

The third question goes directly to his loss condition. What result would make him accept that a rival had captured genuine causality without covertly relying on experiential content, rather than allowing every successful causal vocabulary to be reclassified as an abstraction of experience?

Snyder’s states-of-affairs reformulation adds a nearby question: if the unity of exemplification is the deeper target, what would make experiential unity a better account of that unity than the strongest nonexperiential theory of facts or instantiation?

The fourth asks for a discriminator stronger than gradualism: what should universal prehension make more expected than the strongest gradual process-relational emergentist rival? The fifth asks him to freeze the meaning of valuation long enough for comparison. Does he mean differential response, viability-relative normativity, goal-sensitive selection, intrinsic mattering, attraction and aversion, phenomenal valence, or something else? Evidence for one should not silently travel to the others.

The historical question remains as well. Has the positive content of primitive experience stayed stable across

“experiential quality,”

“like qualia,”

“qualitative what-it-is-like,”

pervasive subjectivity, presubjective inheritance, proto-subjectivity, and the current explicitly non-conscious account? If yes, what definition unifies them without borrowing different implications from each? If no, which part changed? Matt has been very explicit that Whitehead’s basic architecture has not changed, and I believe him. Architecture can remain stable while the interpretation of a contested term becomes more precise.

Finally, the reconstructed-rival question has not gone away. Suppose a non-panexperiential process-relational account preserves real causal influence, inheritance, novelty, organization, agency-like regulation, gradual emergence, and the eventual existence of conscious experience. Would Matt then accept primitive experientiality as underdetermined, or is there some additional target that the rival necessarily failed to preserve? If he can name that target and show why the rival loses it, we finally have the positive residual I have been asking for.

The representational problem matters directly to truth-seeking because a philosophically excellent argument against the substituted rival can still produce the wrong update.

When a public reply attributes a position without quoting it, defeats several claims the original author had already rejected, and then lets those defeats stand in for progress against the live alternative, readers receive the wrong update. They may lower confidence in an argument that was never tested and raise confidence in a conclusion that did not generate the successful criticism. The correction cost then falls disproportionately on the person whose position was reconstructed. A corrigible exchange should do the opposite: preserve the target strongly enough that a successful objection teaches both sides something they did not already believe.

Almost everything Matt spends the reply defending was never the disagreement. The burden is narrower, not gone.

After working through the reply sentence by sentence, the most consequential result is upstream of almost everything else: the question Matt announces as my:

“straightforward question”

is his paraphrase, not a quotation, and the paraphrase introduces the detachable-subtraction and positively nonexperiential framing that the rest of his rebuttal then defeats. Nearly all of the surrounding architecture Matt spends the reply defending was already part of my stated position. I accept the rejection of substance-quality caricatures, the relational constitution of subjects, the distinction between primitive prehension and conscious presentation, the insufficiency of mere Humean succession, the legitimacy of metaphysical and constitutive reasoning, the need to preserve targets rather than win by subtraction, the possibility of gradual development, and the requirement that any demonstrated transition from nonexperience to experience be explained.

Those are substantial claims, but here they are mostly common premises, not positions Matt’s reply forced me to abandon. Their survival strengthens Matt’s broader process-relational case while making the residual easier to see: if causal inheritance, relational becoming, integration, novelty, embodied efficacy, emergent subjects, scale-sensitive organization, and gradual development are available to both sides, success on those dimensions belongs to the shared core.

The specifically Whiteheadian addition still needing comparative warrant is the claim that causal actuality is itself minimally experiential, affective, proto-subjective, perspectival, or valuational.

That also clarifies what Matt’s reply actually establishes. If his case successfully shows that a wholly nonexperiential, dead-mechanistic ontology is inadequate, it defeats a position I did not defend; if it shows that abrupt miraculous emergence is a bad explanation, it defeats another position I did not defend. Those victories remove positions neither of us needs.

They do not choose panexperientialism over the agnostic process-relational alternative I actually offered, because that alternative is precisely the rival his rebuttal keeps replacing. The resulting evidential state is underdetermination among the remaining live alternatives.

Panexperientialism still needs the comparative step that would promote universal experientiality beyond that live rival. My position should move toward Matt if he can show that a reconstructed rival preserving the common ground nevertheless loses some real explanatory, causal, phenomenological, or constitutive target that primitive experientiality uniquely recovers. That would finally attach evidence to the residual instead of to premises we already share.

The evidential bookkeeping can therefore be stated compactly. If some body of evidence supports causal inheritance, relational becoming, integration, novelty, and embodied efficacy under both Matt’s account and its strongest process-relational rival, that evidence belongs first to the shared core. It cannot then be spent a second time as though it independently established the specifically experiential interpretation.

Likewise, if a more extreme rival fails because it asserts primitive nonexperience, dead mechanism, or abrupt miraculous emergence, that failure cannot simply be spent again as evidence for panexperientialism while an agnostic rival that does not make those claims remains standing. Evidence should migrate only across inferential bridges it actually crosses. This is the same discipline that prevented me from turning the success of physical realization into exhaustive physicalism in the essay Matt was responding to.

The comparison has a different geometry from a midpoint between Erik’s physicalism and Matt’s panexperientialism. The strongest empirical center is the physical and organizational dependence we can actually intervene on, together with the process-relational causal structure serious rivals increasingly share.

Around that center sit stronger interpretations - exhaustive physicalism, primitive experientiality, neutral or dual-aspect views, biological necessity claims, and others - whose additional commitments need their own support. Scientific progress can therefore strengthen the common causal and organizational account while simultaneously making the remaining intrinsic-metaphysical dispute narrower and more sharply underdetermined.

That would be progress: we would know more precisely what the evidence establishes and more precisely what it still leaves open.

Seen from that narrower disagreement, Experience Is Not a Predicate still contributes something useful. Matt makes Whitehead’s internal account clearer, states a candidate loss condition, and offers a gradualist expectation that can be compared with developing consciousness science; Ben Snyder’s intervention pushes the internal story toward the unity of states of affairs while explicitly acknowledging that the Whiteheadian answer still needs justification against rival metaphysics.

Those moves make the programme more precise, and that precision should now make the remaining comparison easier to expose.

The article helps rule out some bad ways of denying Whitehead without yet giving us the reason to affirm Whitehead’s experiential interpretation over disciplined suspension of judgment. At the end of all that clarification, the article returns to the same test it began with:

Granting Matt’s process-relational ontology and every point of shared ground, what comparative reason warrants treating causal receptivity itself as intrinsically experiential, affective, proto-subjective, perspectival, or valuational rather than leaving primitive intrinsic character open?

That is the condition under which the distinctively panexperiential part of the programme could finally earn more than the process-relational ground we already share.

And before a response can answer that question, it has to preserve that question rather than replace it with a stronger rival I already excluded. Source fidelity is upstream of rebuttal.

Late addendum while finishing this article: the question survives without my grammar

As I was finishing this response, Jeffrey Williams of After the CollapseCollapse published After Kant and Whitehead: Experience, Metaphysics, and the Remainder, a response to the same Segall essay from a substantially different philosophical position. Williams removes the terminology Matt objected to in my formulation and arrives at almost exactly the same bridge. He asks what warrants extending characteristics encountered within living sensation beyond living organisms and making them characteristics of existence itself. When Matt generalizes embodied feeling into causal connection throughout nature, Williams asks the compact question:

“But why does that follow?”

That convergence functions as a natural wording ablation on the grammatical diagnosis. Remove my word predicate. Remove my RCF vocabulary. Remove my language of analytical subtraction. The comparative problem remains: what warrants carrying features evidenced in living experiential organization into a universal characterization of causal actuality? The question Matt treated as partly generated by my grammar survives when my grammar is removed.

That is the external control predicted by the substitution test above: replace the disputed phrase with neutral comparative language, or remove it entirely, and the same scope question remains.

The convergence occurs upstream of the different answers Williams and I give. Williams pushes much farther toward questioning the metaphysical project itself and toward leaving an irreducible remainder open. My own position permits selective, provisional ontological commitment wherever a proposed entity, relation, mechanism, process, or pattern earns robust comparative leverage. The invariant between us is the bridge question, not a shared metaphysics: what warrants moving from experiential disclosure in living organization to primitive experientiality as a feature of causal actuality generally?

Matt’s public response to Williams makes the triangulation even more useful. He calls Williams’s essay a

“Fantastic reply,”

says they are

“aligned on much,”

and locates a continuing divergence in

“whether metaphysics is avoidable.”

He then argues that factual description and scientific propositions already assume a systematic metaphysical background of some kind. That relocates the disagreement one level upward, but it still leaves several inferential bridges distinct.

Inquiry can begin with background commitments without those commitments already constituting one complete systematic metaphysics. A systematic metaphysical background, even if granted, does not select Whitehead’s system from serious rivals. Selection of Whitehead’s system would still not by itself establish every universal commitment within it. And none of those steps independently establishes that causal actuality as such is intrinsically experiential. Metaphysical non-neutrality does not establish metaphysical completeness, systematicity, or the specific content of a Whiteheadian universal.

Matt’s next observation strengthens the provenance issue rather than weakening it. He writes that

“the ambiguities of language stand inexorably in the way”

of finally articulating a complete systematic background. The more seriously we take that ambiguity, the more important it becomes to preserve the distinction between source wording and framework-supplied interpretation. Quote before paraphrase. Preserve IF as IF. Preserve may as may. Preserve suspension of judgment as suspension of judgment. Mark the point where a technical vocabulary adds structure. Linguistic ambiguity increases the need for inferential provenance.

The Whitehead passage Matt quotes also gives us substantial methodological common ground. Whitehead describes rationalism as an

“experimental adventure,”

“progressive and never final,”

in which partial success still matters. Taken seriously, that is a reason to localize both success and failure. Causal inheritance, relational constitution, becoming, organization, and a critique of vacuous mechanism can remain valuable even if primitive experientiality remains underdetermined. Conversely, if the experiential residual begins generating differential explanatory, predictive, phenomenological, or interventional success, it should gain confidence. An experimental metaphysics should be able to keep what works while allowing the disputed residual to move.

This also separates my question from the disagreement Matt is preparing to have with Williams about metaphysical

“totalitarianism”

or a

“One System to rule them all.”

Open-ended ontology is not the same thing as low-commitment ontology. A metaphysical system can be dynamically unfinished, permanently revisable, and organized around creative advance while still making a strong universal claim that every actuality is experiential. Matt could establish that Whitehead’s system is radically open, non-static, and corrigible, and the bridge question would remain exactly where it was: what warrants that universal experiential characterization rather than the process-relational commitments the strongest rivals already share?

There is one final contrast worth preserving. Matt’s response to Williams models the shape of productive disagreement I have been trying to reach with him: identify the large region of alignment, isolate the remaining divergence, and then ask how and why the residual differs. That is the structure I want here as well.

Grant the shared process-relational core. Isolate primitive experientiality as the remaining disputed commitment. Then test whether anything different follows because it is there.
