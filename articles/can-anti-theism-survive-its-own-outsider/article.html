The Skeptic’s Mirror: necessary truths, social Gods, sophisticated theology, and what happens when Hitchens’ razor cuts both ways

By Nathan Sweet

The same evidential rule should govern supernaturalists, sophisticated theologians, and skeptics: isolate the added commitment, make it earn discriminating evidence, and state what would force revision.

My recent public debate with Aron Ra, an American Atheists Board Member and self-described antitheist, became a test of whether skeptical criticism helps build that stronger comparison. Aron himself recognizes the wider target: “The Bible is easy to disprove, but that shouldn’t be enough to disprove God.” This article asks what follows methodologically from that recognition.

Aron’s input was very helpful, and I appreciate his willingness to test my argument. The exchange exposed weaknesses and helped me improve it. I welcome further comments and criticisms that could lead to similar improvements, including challenges to the revisions presented here.

I’ve spent the last few days since my public debate with Aron Ra going back over the scholarship and applying my own critical methodology to my own argument, as well as Aron Ra’s responses to that argument. My aim is to build the strongest naturalistic counter-model I can, together with a methodology for evaluating metaphysical claims said to have implications in our shared reality. Religious experiences, traditions, institutions, and moral commitments give us phenomena to explain. What, if anything, warrants the further claim that a supernatural agent, revelation, divine purpose, or universal experiential ground explains them better?

That project matters especially when potentially harmful forms of theism survive the rejection of biblical literalism and creationism. A theology can accept evolution and deep time while claiming authority over conscience, bodies, education, or public policy. Intellectual sophistication does not establish that authority. Nor does the possibility of harm make a theology false: the evidential case and the ethical assessment each need their own argument.

By a naturalistic null hypothesis, I mean a provisional family of explanatory comparators using biological, psychological, historical, and social processes without assuming the disputed theological addition. This is a research baseline, not yet a statistical null with specified parameters, and it does not assume that naturalism exhausts reality. Its job is to make a comparison possible: what should differ if the addition is true, how would we distinguish that difference from bias or error, and what result would make either account lose support?

The challenge therefore reaches beyond requesting more evidence. We need a method that explains why a finding favors one account over its serious alternatives, and how that judgment can be corrected. If a claim has no specified empirical consequences, its conceptual or explanatory case must still be assessed without quietly crediting it with evidence that competing accounts accommodate equally well.

I do not use atheist as a global identity label; the Stanford Encyclopedia distinguishes global from local atheisms. My judgments concern specified claims. Where a supernatural hypothesis predicts detectable effects that fail to appear, and naturalistic alternatives explain the observations better, I can judge it probably false. An underspecified or empirically indistinguishable claim instead leaves less basis for that judgment. Neither the word God nor its rejection settles every referent in advance.

The title’s “outsider test” comes from John W. Loftus’s The Outsider Test for Faith: evaluate an inherited religion with the skepticism you would apply to a rival religion. My extension rotates the test once more. Apply the same skeptical standards to skeptical claims when the skeptic becomes the claimant.

The immediate disagreement concerned a broad evidential claim Aron used to support a public-policy conclusion. He wrote:

“As there is absolutely no actual factual evidence of nor any logically valid arguments either, but plenty of both against, then it is irresponsible and dishonest for the State to promote policies pretending as if some particular religion is true.”

A defensible opposition to state endorsement of religion does not require proving every religious argument invalid. The additional claims, “absolutely no actual factual evidence,” “no logically valid arguments,” and “plenty of both against,” each require their own support. Once a skeptic offers them as reasons, they become subject to the same scrutiny as any other claim.

The recursive standard is not one I invented for Aron. I reminded him of several standards he had previously used against religious arguments: a claim is not truth merely because someone asserts or believes it and cannot be called truth until we can “SHOW that it is true”; “The only value information has is however accurate you can show it to be,” otherwise “It is just an empty assertion”; and, after previously recognizing that an inference he regarded as obvious had not been made explicit enough for his audience, “I must learn to be more explicit in my explanations.” I also quoted his criticism of a presuppositionalist for offering “no evidence whatsoever; only the logical fallacy of a non-sequitur.”

Those earlier statements supply the mirror from Aron’s own side of the glass: assertions require demonstration, a non-sequitur must be shown rather than named, and “obvious” is not a substitute for making the inferential work explicit.

Aron has also stated a closely related evidential standard. In his November 22, 2015 post, “Anti-theist Answers to Christian Questions”, answering question 25 about sufficient evidence for God, he wrote: “I’ll accept whatever qualifies as evidence. That means a body of objectively verifiable facts that are positively indicative of and/or exclusively concordant with that one conclusion over any other.” In answer 15 of the same post, he also wrote, “If God were real, there would be a way to him through science too.” Those are demanding discrimination criteria. The mirror I am using here therefore does not ask Aron to accept a foreign evidential rule. It asks whether the same demand for objectively verifiable, conclusion-discriminating evidence applies when his own universal skeptical diagnoses become the claims under examination.

Our disagreement therefore concerned what happens when skeptical standards are aimed inward at a skeptical conclusion. The immediate dispute became whether a contingent premise can be true and usable in a sound deductive argument. The larger question is harder: what skeptical method still works after the easy biblical targets are gone?

A flood claim can be tested against geology. A six-thousand-year-old Earth can be tested against physics, astronomy, and paleontology. A prophecy can be tested against textual history. Sophisticated theology may accept evolution, deep time, textual development, metaphorical scripture, and ordinary causal closure while adding mind, purpose, value, universal experience, divine ground, or cosmic meaning at a more abstract level. Those additions still owe evidence.

I began by trying to make Aron’s anti-theist argument harder to attack.

I wrote:

“saying no God argument can be valid is too broad; the stronger criticism is that supernatural and religion-specific conclusions go well beyond what the premises have actually earned.

This is much more damaging to theism because it concedes that valid arguments are easy to construct and forces the real burden onto the only question that matters: whether the premises actually justify a supernatural, personal, revelatory, religion-specific God.”

That remains my position. Validity is cheap compared with establishing the premises and every bridge required to reach a substantive conclusion.

Aron objected to the modal force of my paraphrase and clarified his own claim:

“I didn’t say that no god argument CAN be valid. I said that every argument for God that there currently is or ever was is invalid, or at least faulty.”

The distinction is real. My phrase “can be valid” was modal; his stated claim was historical and universal: every argument that currently exists or ever existed is defective. That clarification narrows what I may attribute to him. It does not erase the separate unrestricted wording quoted in the introduction, “no logically valid arguments.” The charitable reading is that he meant no existing argument succeeds, not that a valid God-argument is modally impossible.

Aron continued:

“As they all rely on frauds, falsehoods and fallacies, and their syllogisms have premises that are not necessarily true along with conclusions that are always non-sequitur. When I tell them there is no logically valid argument, I expect them to push back with an example. But they don’t. They won’t because they know they can’t. Because it is all make-believe and the wanna-pretenders know that I have already seen all the best mental contortions that their raving inanity could produce.”

That statement combines distinct defects. According to the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s account of validity and soundness, a deductive argument is valid when it is impossible for its premises to be true and its conclusion false. As philosopher Catarina Dutilh Novaes puts it: “A valid argument whose premises are also true is said to be sound.” A false premise can make an argument unsound without making it invalid. Fraud concerns provenance or honesty. A fallacy is a defect in reasoning. A non sequitur is an inferential failure.

I therefore replied:

“False or unsupported premises make an argument unsound, not invalid; fraud would be a provenance problem; and a non sequitur is specifically a validity problem. Those are worth keeping separate because otherwise a believer can correctly point out the technical mistake and dodge the stronger criticism.”

My wording needs a correction too: an unsupported premise does not necessarily make an argument unsound. It leaves its soundness unestablished on the evidence offered; the premise might nevertheless be true. A false premise makes a deductive argument unsound.

The opening rhetoric also matters. Calling prospective respondents “wanna-pretenders” producing “raving inanity” does not bear on their premises or inferences. It functions as poisoning the well if it invites the audience to classify contrary arguments psychologically before evaluating them. The IEP fallacy survey distinguishes defects in an argument from attacks on an arguer or motive. A fallacy weakens an inferential route; it does not establish the opposite conclusion.

My first argument specified a historical-social referent.

Aron said:

“When I tell them there is no logically valid argument, I expect them to push back with an example.”

So I supplied one:

P1. Any materially instantiated higher-level social pattern with reproducible causal effects exists as a social entity.
P2. The historical-social God is such a pattern.
C. Therefore the historical-social God exists.

I wrote:

“That is valid, and its premises are at least plausibly sound. It establishes no supernatural deity whatsoever, but it is enough to defeat ‘every argument for God that currently exists or ever existed is invalid or unsound’ unless you stipulate in advance that by ‘God’ you specifically mean a supernatural personal being.”

The referent was explicit from the start: “the historical-social God,” with “no supernatural deity whatsoever” established by the conclusion. Nothing in this inference changes a social tradition into a supernatural being. Aron could question whether that tradition satisfies P1, or whether an argument about it addresses his intended claim. Those are different objections.

My description of the premises as “plausibly sound” does need correction. Soundness belongs to an argument, not its individual premises. I also explicitly denied that mere plausibility establishes soundness. The original inference is valid when its stated referent is held fixed; whether its premises are true and adequately supported is a separate question. Validity alone does not defeat the broader claim that an argument is “invalid or unsound.”

The distinction between minimal social existence and stronger explanatory leverage also deserves a separate formulation. My original P1 required reproducible causal effects; the surrounding discussion proposed comparative tests for higher-level explanatory status. In the debate I explicitly said that material evidence did not by itself prove all of P2. The minimal existence claim can be stated independently:

P1m. Any materially realized and historically identifiable social tradition-complex exists at least as a historical-social pattern.
P2m. At least some genealogically bounded YHWH/God tradition-complex is materially realized and historically identifiable.
Cm. Therefore at least some YHWH/God tradition-complex exists as a historical-social pattern.

That conclusion is deliberately modest. It does not establish a supernatural deity, a unified macro-agent, or irreducible higher-level causation. It only identifies a historical-social referent.

This answers an unrestricted “no valid argument” claim only if that claim includes the stated social referent. It does not refute the claim that no sound argument has established an independently existing supernatural God. I made that scope qualification in my first reply. Aron could reasonably exclude social-existence arguments from his intended theological target; that would delimit the disagreement, not show this inference invalid or its social premise false.

A stronger and separate research programme asks whether some YHWH/God lineage structure earns nonredundant higher-level realist treatment. That requires additional criteria and evidence, and it remains open below.

Then one phrase, “not necessarily true,” took over the debate.

Aron responded:

“Thanks for the example to prove my point that every religious syllogism starts with premises that are not necessarily true.”

I answered the inference immediately rather than merely asking for clarification:

“you didn’t actually show that my example proves your point, because you never identified either premise as false or inadequately supported; you only observed that they are ‘not necessarily true,’ which either means merely contingent, and is irrelevant to soundness, or means unsupported, in which case you still have to show where the support fails.”

I also gave the stronger higher-level claim an explicit loss condition: demote it if lower-level psychological and social variables preserve the same explanatory, historical, predictive, or intervention work. And I noted that using this one case to support the universal claim that every religious syllogism begins with defective premises would itself require a further argument rather than follow from the example.

“Not necessarily true” has a standard modal meaning. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy on modality treats necessity and contingency as modal notions and separately discusses epistemic modality in terms of what the available evidence leaves open. A contingent proposition can be actually true while failing to be necessary.

I asked:

“when you say my premises are ‘not necessarily true,’ do you mean contingent, false, or inadequately supported?”

Each answer has different consequences. Contingency is not a defect in soundness. Actual falsity defeats soundness if the premise is required. Inadequate support creates an evidential burden.

Aron returned:

“Are we still talking syllogisms where the premises are not necessarily true?”

I replied:

“We are talking about whether the premises are true and warranted, not whether they are ‘necessarily true.’ ... A sound deductive argument needs a valid inference and true premises, not premises that are true in every possible world.”

I used evolution as a reductio. A concrete experimental example strengthens that point: Zachary Blount, Christina Borland, and Richard Lenski (2008) replayed evolution from stored E. coli clones to investigate why citrate use evolved in one population. Earlier and later clones differed in their tendency to evolve the innovation. Their conclusion was that “the evolution of this phenotype was contingent on the particular history of that population.” Historical dependence was something to investigate, not a reason to disqualify the finding. This experiment does not settle metaphysical determinism; it illustrates how science studies outcomes dependent on particular antecedent conditions.

Aron then wrote:

“If the premises aren’t true, then the syllogism can’t act actually prove anything. Having a ‘plausible’ premise at best means ‘maybe’. But when neither of your premises are necessarily true, then it doesn’t work at all.”

The first sentence concerns truth and soundness. Read modally, the last imposes a further necessity requirement. From:

It is not necessary that P.

we do not get “P is false.” We get only that P is not true in every relevant possible world. The disputed move is:

possibly false
therefore actually unusable.

As a general rule about deductive arguments, that inference is false. Consider: all objects in this box are metal; this coin is in the box; therefore this coin is metal. In any actual case where both premises are true, the argument is sound, although neither premise had to be true. Necessity belongs to the truth-preserving connection, not to each empirical premise.

If Aron instead means “not established as true,” he has a legitimate question about warrant. But that reading requires evaluating the support for these premises; it does not turn contingency into a logical defect.

There is a separate scientific comparison if “may be false” means that some uncertainty remains. The LIGO and Virgo discovery paper (2016) reported gravitational-wave detection with an estimated false-alarm rate below one event per 203,000 years and supplied uncertainty intervals for inferred source properties. That false-alarm rate is not the probability that the conclusion is false. It shows how a discovery can be warranted through explicit error assessment without becoming infallible. This is an example of empirical inference, not a claim that uncertain premises yield deductive certainty.

Applied consistently, a rule excluding contingent premises would disqualify empirical premises science routinely uses; a rule excluding all residual uncertainty would disqualify warranted scientific conclusions. These are different rules. Neither follows from the legitimate demand to establish the premises of a sound deduction. The criticism is of those rules if asserted, not an attribution to Aron of a wish to reject science.

I pointed out the shift:

“you just shifted from ‘not necessarily true’ to ‘if the premises aren’t true’ and then back again to ‘neither of your premises are necessarily true.’ I agree that if a premise is actually false, the syllogism is unsound, but that was never the dispute.”

Aron replied:

“I didn’t shift anything. Being not necessarily true leaves open that either or both may be false. I shouldn’t have to explain this to a philosopher!”

A contingent proposition may be false. It may also be true. The proposed general disqualification is refuted by sound arguments with contingent premises. What remains unresolved is whether Aron accepts that distinction and which evidential objection he intends.

“I shouldn’t have to explain this to a philosopher” invokes my standing to portray an undemonstrated error as an embarrassing failure of competence. Its ad hominem force is to invite readers to discount me and my further replies while the disputed inference remains unproved. That is the well-poisoning concern: the alleged error becomes grounds for personal discredit before it has been established. The same failure would occur if a theist dismissed Aron’s objection as ignorance he ought to have outgrown. Neither judgment of the speaker supplies the missing argument.

My answer remains:

“Of course a contingent premise could have been false; soundness asks whether it is true, not whether it is true in every possible world.”

Before Aron moved to the later “word salad” objection, I also explicitly ruled out the weaker positions his replies could otherwise be read as attributing to me:

“I am not claiming these premises are necessary truths, that mere plausibility makes an argument sound, that possibility is evidence, or that this syllogism establishes a supernatural God.”

That matters because my later correction from “plausibly sound” to a more exact evidential description does not concede that I had argued plausibility was sufficient for soundness. I had already denied that inference in the thread.

At that point I reduced the dispute to four discriminating questions.

Which premise fails? Which condition does it fail? What evidence demonstrates the failure? What evidence or argument would make the diagnosis change?

Aron later answered the first two: P2 was the target, and he said it failed every stated condition. Aron did not demonstrate every alleged failure or state what would make him revise his diagnosis. The modal disqualification fails as a general rule; acknowledgment of that error remains absent. Appendix B summarizes the questions and responses.

Aron then made more specific claims, each requiring a separate test.

Aron later wrote:

“OK, so once we remove the word salad in the dubious assertion that is Premise One, then we can summarize it as: ‘If it’s real, it’s real’.

There is no second premise.

What is called Premise Two ignores the fact that God is not a social pattern at any level, nor is it materially instantiated, nor reliably identifiable, and has no reproducible explanatory, historical, predictive, or causal leverage; none of that at all. But but believers still claim all of those things, because faith causes them to lie about everything.

Thus Premise Two isn’t even a premise; it’s just a non-sequitur jump to an assumed conclusion.”

That response contains distinct claims: P1 is circular or meaningless; P2 is not a premise; the relevant pattern is not social, materially instantiated, or identifiable; it has no explanatory, historical, predictive, or causal leverage; and the conclusion is a non sequitur.

I answered the P1 criticism by making the criterion more explicit:

“P1 does not say ‘if it’s real, it’s real.’ It says that if a higher-level social pattern is materially instantiated, independently identifiable, and earns reproducible explanatory, historical, predictive, or causal leverage, then that is sufficient reason to treat it as real at that social scale.”

I then operationalized the disputed terms rather than leaving them as labels:

“‘materially instantiated’ means there are observable people, artifacts, practices, representations, institutions, and processes realizing the pattern. ‘Reliably identifiable’ means we can specify why different manifestations count as instances or historical continuations of that pattern rather than grouping them arbitrarily. And ‘explanatory, historical, predictive, or causal leverage’ means retaining the higher-level pattern helps us explain, reconstruct, predict, or intervene better than an adequate rival description that leaves it out.”

So reducing the criterion to “if it’s real, it’s real” does not expose a circularity unless those proposed tests themselves collapse. It deletes the tests before evaluating them. I also gave Aron three explicit ways to defeat or force revision of the argument: show that the P1 criterion is inadequate; show that the historical-social YHWH/God pattern fails one of P2’s stated conditions; or show that the conclusion does not follow even if P1 and P2 are true. Aron then selected the second route and said P2 failed every condition.

That selection makes a condition-by-condition comparison available. It does not establish that Aron conceded meaningfulness: a critic can test a criterion for the sake of argument while separately disputing it. The two objections must nevertheless be distinguished and demonstrated.

The later formulation makes independent identifiability explicit and broadens the kinds of leverage under consideration. It develops the original criterion rather than repeating it word for word. Comparative explanatory tests were already present in my earlier reply. Aron’s initial paraphrase must be assessed against the wording he was answering; the separate formulations below clarify which burdens belong to minimal existence and which to stronger realism.

Even the original P1 named material realization and reproducible effects. “If it’s real, it’s real” omits those proposed grounds and leaves only an empty conclusion. That is a substantive misrepresentation of the proposed criterion. The later operational definitions make the difference explicit. They do not prove the criterion correct, but repeating “meaningless” does not test them. Nor would showing that an existence criterion is partly analytic establish that an empirical application of it is meaningless. To demonstrate circularity, the critic must identify where the conclusion is assumed in establishing the premises. To challenge the criterion instead, the critic can show that its tests admit arbitrary groupings or fail to support the intended realist conclusion. Those are substantive possibilities; deleting the tests does not establish either.

The serious question inside Aron’s criticism survives: do those conditions actually distinguish robust higher-level social patterns from arbitrary descriptions? If they do not, P1′ fails.

He then made a different claim: “There is no second premise.”

Aron later made the premise-status claim explicit:

“P2 is literally not even a premise. For the reason that it is actually a reaction of the type that is typical of believers. Whatever criteria is given to accept something as real, apologists will automatically say that their god meets whatever that criteria is.”

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy on argument treats premises by their inferential role. If P2 is offered with P1 in support of C, it functions as a premise. Truth, support, ideological origin, and motive are separate questions. This was also my answer in the exchange itself:

“P2 is also a premise even if you believe it is false. A false premise makes an argument unsound; it does not make the premise disappear or turn the inference into a non sequitur.”

If P2 is false, the deduction is unsound. If P2 assumes C, it may beg the question. If “God” changes meaning, the argument may equivocate. If P1 and P2 can both be true while C is false, the inference is invalid. “Typical of believers” does not determine premise-status. The stated reason fails to support the stated conclusion. P2 functions as a premise whether its content ultimately survives scrutiny or not.

Consider an illustrative syllogism: all planets move in perfect circles; Mars is a planet; therefore Mars moves in a perfect circle. The first premise is false—Kepler’s work on Mars helped replace circular orbits with ellipses—but it remains a premise, and the inference remains valid. Rejecting a scientific assertion changes its evidential standing; it does not erase its role in an argument. The same distinction applies to religious assertions.

The move from ideological typicality to “not a premise” is a non sequitur. If ideological origin is also being used to establish falsity, that is a genetic fallacy. The IEP also notes the relevant nuance: provenance can affect evidential weight and justify verification. It does not replace evaluation of the claim itself. As I put the distinction in the thread, suspicious provenance can justify independent verification, but “people of this ideological type often say things like this” does not yield “therefore this proposition is not a premise,” still less “therefore it is false.”

Aron’s further generalization,

“Whatever criteria is given ... apologists will automatically say that their god meets whatever that criteria is.”

may describe a real apologetic strategy, but as a claim about all apologists it requires evidence and still does not tell us whether this P2 satisfies these criteria.

The stronger statement,

“faith causes them to lie about everything”

is a hasty generalization and poisoning-the-well move if used to discredit the premise. A proposition’s truth does not depend on the team associated with it.

Aron later repeated that P2 was “literally not even a premise,” so I do not count premise-status as an implicit concession. But he never answered the inferential-role point: no rule was supplied by which ideological typicality makes a declarative proposition offered in support of C cease functioning as a premise. Aron also offered no evidence for “faith causes them to lie about everything.” That assertion supplies no established reason to reject P2.

An objection about a supernatural being does not answer the stated social referent.

When Aron wrote,

“God is not a social pattern at any level, nor is it materially instantiated”

there are two possible readings. If he meant the supernatural being asserted by believers, he was denying a claim my first argument expressly excluded. If he meant the historical-social pattern I specified, he was making substantive denials about its realization and organization. Either reading needs to be assessed against that stated referent.

I reiterated the distinction:

“When you say ‘God is not materially instantiated,’ you seem to be talking about the supernatural being believers claim exists. I agree that my argument has not established such a being or force. P2 refers instead to the historically documented YHWH/God tradition: its texts, representations, rituals, institutions, identities, laws, communities, and behaviors.”

I then gave the objection its strongest form rather than shielding P2 from criticism:

“if you are saying that those realizers do not form a sufficiently coherent historical-social pattern, or that keeping track of that pattern adds nothing beyond simply listing the people, texts, institutions, and behaviors involved, that is a substantive objection to P2 and exactly the kind of criticism I am asking for. But if you are saying that a supernatural God is not materially instantiated, then you are denying a claim my argument deliberately does not make.”

Aron’s later wording removes any doubt about the target of that objection. He explicitly named “The historical-social YHWH/God pattern” and said it failed every condition in P1. At that point he was directly challenging the social model, so it would be inaccurate to say he never engaged the stated referent. The remaining question is whether his denials were demonstrated. Appealing to the absence of a supernatural being could not establish them.

I also made the label optional:

“You do not have to call the historical-social pattern ‘God,’ and my argument does not depend on claiming that this pattern is numerically identical to the supernatural being a theist means by ‘God.’ Call it the historical-social YHWH/God tradition-complex if that is clearer.”

That wording protects the referent specified from the outset. It closes the objection that the argument depends on moving between social and supernatural meanings of “God.” The supernatural claim remains a further proposition requiring additional evidence. Questions about whether the social pattern is well bounded or explanatorily useful remain open, but substituting a supernatural being for that pattern cannot answer them.

A higher-level social pattern can be physically realized through components and relations without being an extra fundamental substance.

Contemporary social ontology makes that question legitimate. In a peer-reviewed 2023 paper, Francesco Guala discusses an inferentialist approach in which real kinds are “real-world structures that make successful scientific predictions, explanations, and interventions possible,” specifically considering historical and multi-functional social kinds. Brian Epstein’s peer-reviewed framework separates the grounding of social facts from the anchoring of social categories and notes that it has become “intellectually respectable to take social properties seriously.”

Neither proves P1′. They show that material realization, higher-level social reality, multiple realization, explanatory leverage, grounding, and category anchoring are substantive philosophical questions rather than empty vocabulary.

My Provisional, Boundary- and Scale-Relative Realism makes the commitment graded: treat a higher-level pattern as increasingly real to the extent that it earns robust prediction, intervention, explanation, or transfer at a specified boundary, and demote it if an adequate rival preserves that work without the pattern.

Aron made an empirical denial that can be checked.

Aron eventually stated:

“The historical-social YHWH/God pattern fails every one of the stated conditions in P1.”

Earlier:

“God is not a social pattern at any level, nor is it materially instantiated, nor reliably identifiable, and has no reproducible explanatory, historical, predictive, or causal leverage; none of that at all.”

Those are strong empirical denials. They also have a simple quantifier structure that matters to the burden of rebuttal. Aron said the pattern fails every one of the conditions and has none of that at all. I therefore did not need to establish every part of the stronger P2′ programme to falsify that universal formulation. One established property among those explicitly denied is enough to defeat “every one” and “none.” The rest of the evidence asks how many of the remaining denials survive.

First, material realization can be checked against the historical record.

Jeremy Smoak and William Schniedewind’s peer-reviewed study of Kuntillet ʿAjrud analyzes eighth-century BCE inscriptions while explicitly resisting the inference that their religious content, by itself, establishes a cultic function for the site. They instead place divine names and blessing formulas within daily life and Israelian scribal curriculum. One Pithos A inscription includes:

“I have [b]lessed you to YHWH of Shomron (Samaria) and to his ʾasherah.”

That does not establish a supernatural Yahweh. It establishes material traces of YHWH naming, blessing formulas, representation, and scribal practice. At the minimal historical-social boundary specified here, these artifacts contradict the denial of material realization. Whether they form a sufficiently coherent, nonredundant macro-level kind is a further question. Rejecting that stronger claim cannot erase the material practices themselves.

Second, historical identifiability can be treated as a genealogical problem.

Religious traditions transform, split, hybridize, and retrospectively rewrite themselves. I explicitly rejected an immutable essence in the debate and treated a coarse or fractured genealogy as a reason to revise the model. The issue is how well specified lineages can be tracked through change.

But difficult identity through change is not the same as untrackable lineage. In 2025, Anastasia Ejova and colleagues used computational phylogenetic methods on a purpose-built dataset of 291 religious denominations and their genealogical relationships, reconstructing dated Indo-Iranian, Islamic, and Judeo-Christian phylogenies.

That study supplies a concrete genealogical model to assess; it does not by its existence prove the model reliable or establish my exact partition. Aron’s specific denial must be tested against the relevant lineage evidence, rather than enlarged into a claim about all religious genealogy. The stronger form of P2′ should therefore concern a nested, genealogically structured family of YHWH/God lineages, with uncertainty represented explicitly.

Third, predictive and explanatory leverage are empirical questions too.

Aron’s claim of “no reproducible explanatory, historical, predictive, or causal leverage; none of that at all” is also testable.

A 2019 meta-analysis by Henk Stulp, Jurrijn Koelen, Annemiek Schep-Akkerman, Gerrit Glas, and Liesbeth Eurelings-Bontekoe was based on 123 independent samples from 112 primary studies and 348 effect sizes. The authors report that “narrowing down the general concept of religiosity to specific measures of God representations resulted in stronger associations,” while also reporting substantial heterogeneity across results.

A cross-cultural study by Benjamin Purzycki and colleagues tested 591 participants from eight communities. Holding relevant variables constant, higher ratings of gods as punitive and knowledgeable were associated with greater allocations to geographically distant co-religionists relative to oneself and local co-religionists.

These studies support associations involving measurable God representations. They do not by themselves establish causal direction, held-out predictive improvement, or the incremental contribution of an entire historical lineage. If Aron’s denial encompasses those representation-level associations, this is relevant counterevidence. If his target is specifically nonredundant lineage-level leverage, that stronger question remains open. The material evidence already defeats “every condition fails”; the behavioral evidence need not be stretched to do more than it establishes.

The stronger research claim retains its own evidential burden.

The stronger research question I take from the exchange is:

Your P2′ is a genuine premise, but you still owe evidence that the historically connected YHWH/God tradition-complex is sufficiently coherent, sufficiently well-bounded, and sufficiently nonredundant to merit realist treatment as a higher-level social pattern rather than serving as shorthand for lower-level psychological, textual, institutional, demographic, and network variables.

I accept that burden for the stronger research claim. Accepting it does not rehabilitate the modal error, the premise-status error, or the blanket denial of material realization. In particular, I do not need to establish nonredundant macro-level causation to show that an identified tradition has material realizers.

A critic can reasonably withhold belief pending that stronger evidence without building an alternative model. That research question remains open; Aron did not demonstrate a refutation of the stronger model.

The claim requires evidence about the specified pattern and comparisons that could support or defeat it.

The appropriate test is a cost-matched model comparison.

Model 1: people, texts, institutions, rituals, incentives, psychological variables, networks, demographics, and local representations, with no higher-level historical lineage variable.

Model 2: the same variables plus explicitly modeled historical descent, transmission, branching, inherited constraints, and YHWH/God lineage structure.

Specify the outcome, historical boundary, competing partitions, and comparison criteria before evaluating success. Compare held-out reconstruction and prediction across settings; assess causal claims with designs that can identify causal effects. Match data access and account for model complexity so extra variables cannot win merely by fitting more noise. Ablations and robustness checks should test whether lineage structure supplies the gain.

If Model 1 preserves the work, demote the stronger lineage claim. If Model 2 repeatedly improves performance under those controls, increase confidence in its usefulness and investigate what realist interpretation that success warrants. Improved prediction alone does not establish an autonomous agent or irreducible causal power. This test of macro-level realism is also separate from using ordinary naturalistic explanations as theological comparators; those explanations need not wait for P1′ to succeed.

That is the research programme developed from the comparative tests I proposed in the debate.

A successful historical-social model makes the supernatural addition earn its support.

The aim is to develop a naturalistic rival against which supernatural explanations can be compared.

Let:

H: a naturalistic model of nested historical-social YHWH/God lineages together with their cognitive, textual, institutional, ritual, and behavioral realizers.

G: an additional independently existing supernatural agent corresponding to some theological interpretation of those lineages.

Texts, institutions, rituals, religious experiences, political structures, moral systems, conversions, schisms, prayers, laws, art, wars, communities, behavioral effects, and historical transmission are evidence of something. The skeptical question is not simply:

Is there evidence for God?

It is:

Evidence of what?

If H already makes a body of evidence E probable, E cannot simply be credited wholesale to H+G. Richard Carrier’s Bayesian treatment of religious arguments is useful here because the relevant issue is comparative likelihood under rival hypotheses.

The discriminating quantity is the likelihood ratio:

P(E | H + G) / P(E | H).

For specified models, a ratio near one means E contributes little likelihood evidence for adding G beyond H. Posterior odds also depend on priors, and predictions must account for auxiliary assumptions and parameter uncertainty. H and H+G are schematic labels here; no numerical Bayes factor has been calculated. Evidence can favor G without being logically exclusive to G, but merely showing that G accommodates it does not establish that advantage.

The supernaturalist therefore owes a residual: what does G explain better, what prediction changes, what observation should differ, and what independent information channel, intervention, or held-out success discriminates H+G from H?
