In short: Matt Segall admits he collapsed my conditional argument and agnosticism into a position I never held. This article asks what remains of the critique built on that misattribution, and explains why my earlier, detailed response was a provenance record rather than a list of demands.

Why my previous response was so detailed

Before returning to our ongoing debate, I want to make one thing clear about my previous response to the Whiteheadian process philosopher Matthew David Segall’s reply to my argument: “Experience Is Not a Predicate”: I Never Made the Argument He Rebutted. My Question Remains Unanswered. Which in turn was in part Matt’s response to my article: I Wanted Erik Svensson’s Argument for Physicalism to Be Stronger. So I Tried to Build One Myself. that he posted here: Experience Is Not a Predicate

I did not write that long, meticulous response because I expected Matt to answer every paragraph, citation, or point in kind. It was not an assignment for him. It was a provenance record.

The problem I was trying to solve was that my argument kept changing as it passed through paraphrase. A conditional became a commitment. Agnosticism became nonexperience. An analytical comparison became an ontology. A request for a discriminator became a defense of the rival Matt already knew how to defeat. Once that happens repeatedly, a short denial is not enough, because the next response can simply generate another nearby attribution and the disagreement moves again.

So I put the wording side by side. I preserved the IFs and the mays. I traced which claims were quotations, which were paraphrases, which inferences depended on which substitutions, and which positions I had already excluded before Matt wrote his rebuttal. I even reconstructed the strongest version of Matt’s underlying argument rather than treating my interpretation of it as another quotation from him.

The point of all that detail was not to make Matt reproduce it. The point was to make continued confusion harder to produce.

I wanted a stable public record against which any future attribution, including my own, could simply be checked.

That is also why the present article is shorter. The documentary work has already been done. What remains is to ask what Matt’s admission changes, what it has not yet repaired, and whether the substantive Whiteheadian claim can finally be compared with the position I actually hold.

Matthew David Segall has now acknowledged the central attribution error in his response to me. He writes that he “did collapse the two-way conditional structure” of my argument and my “explicit agnosticism regarding the primitive intrinsic character of causality.” He also says that this collapse should have been identified as his own argumentative move rather than attributed to me.

I appreciate the admission.

But an admission is not yet a repair.

The problem was not that an obscure qualification buried deep in a long paper was missed. The two-way structure was in the opening argument of the physicalism essay Matt responded to, and I stated it again near the beginning of my response to his “Experience Is Not a Predicate” article. I had also been pressing the same problem with the experiential/nonexperiential binary before either of those pieces, including explicitly asking whether my own factorization might be wrong because there may be no phenomenally neutral causal carrier beneath experiential actuality.

That matters because the position Matt spent much of his article criticizing was not a commitment hidden somewhere in my prose. It was produced by turning one conditional branch into my ontology.

A conditional is not an ontology.

What I actually argued

My argument was deliberately two-way.

If primitive feeling adds nothing beyond causal inheritance, receptivity, transition, integration, responsiveness, novelty, and the process-relational structure both accounts already accept, then a lower-commitment process account may recover much of the same work while leaving primitive intrinsic character unresolved.

If feeling contributes some irreducible affective, subjective, valuational, or otherwise experiential character, then that difference is substantive and needs warrant.

I was not asserting the first branch. I was asking what discriminates the branches.

I also explicitly left open a third possibility: perhaps the factorization itself fails. Perhaps there is no phenomenally neutral causal carrier that can even be analytically separated from feeling without distortion. If that is Matt’s position, I have repeatedly asked him to make that case.

Analytical distinguishability is not ontological separability.

Temporarily holding shared commitments fixed so that rival theories can be compared does not assert that those commitments exist independently in reality. Testing a decomposition is not presupposing that the decomposition succeeds.

This is not a new clarification invented after Matt’s critique. It is the logic of the argument he was responding to.

The revised attribution still changes my position

Matt’s acknowledgment removes one error and then introduces a nearby one. He now says my factorization “treats causal uptake, integration, transition, response, etc. as a common core that can be rendered intelligible independently of feeling.”

Nathan’s request for an erratum is fair. I haven’t read the entire reply he has shared, since I just don’t have the time right now. But I want to admit that I did collapse the two-way conditional structure of his argument, and his explicit agnosticism regarding the primitive intrinsic character of causality. I should have made that collapse explicit as my own argumentative move rather than risk misattributing the resulting position to him.

That said, there remains a substantive disagreement that Nathan himself recognizes when he asks whether the decomposition between experiential and non-experiential understandings of causal relation is finally coherent. I do not think it is finally coherent to sever affectivity from causal relation, even hypothetically. My objection is not that Nathan has chosen the nonexperiential variant, but that the factorization already treats causal uptake, integration, transition, response, etc, as a common core that can be rendered intelligible independently of feeling.

My argument that “experience is not a predicate” was trying to get at this issue. I accept Nathan’s point that his argument does not depend on Whitehead’s technical definition of that term. But my proposal is not that an antecedently intelligible causal relation possesses an additional experiential feature, but that concretely considered, causal relation just is felt inheritance of the past into the present.

Schelling made a convergent point against Newtonian physicists in his 1797 text Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature: “You can in no way make intelligible what a force might be independent of you. For force as such makes itself known only to your feeling.” He goes on to say that physics nevertheless makes objective use of the concept of feeling in its explanations of causal relations. This does not by itself prove nature is experiential, but does make the case that the supposedly objective concept of causal force derives its concrete intelligibility from embodied feeling.

Now, I agree with Nathan that this identification of bodily feeling and causal relation a such bears a comparative burden with alternative metaphysical accounts of causation. But my identity claim is not offered as the detection of an additional feature. Rather, it competes abductively with rival accounts of what causality concretely is. Here is where Nathan’s epistemic asymmetry between physicalism and panexperientialism breaks down. A “physical” description gives us abstract structural dynamical relations, whereas embodied experience provides the one case in which causal efficacy is disclosed directly from within. Again, this doesn’t necessarily prove the universal extent of experiential relations, but it does provide an evidential basis for asking whether the supposedly experientially neutral causal core is itself an abstraction from something more concrete.

So while the comparative burden is one I take seriously, there is no scheme-neutral standpoint that might vindicate one scheme over another (as Nathan himself also seems to admit). The relative verification of a scheme is sought in its general success in elucidating experience across the various domains of human life (including but not limited to scientific investigation), and not in its peculiar certainty or initial clarity of first principles. Rival schemes are experimental matrices of categories whose warrant is determined through comparison of their coherence, adequacy, applicability, and avoidance of misplaced concreteness. We cannot start our comparison of schemes by assuming an experience-neutral common ground of causality in relation to which the rivals might be judged, since whether such a concept of causality is legitimate or already too abstract is precisely what is in dispute.

So I grant that Nathan was not positively asserting feelingless causality. My question is whether causality can even be rendered hypothetically in a concretely intelligible way after feeling has been analytically bracketed out. I take Whitehead’s panexperiential proposal to be a fallible but evidentially motivated speculative generalization, not an experiential predicate arbitrarily added to an otherwise complete causal description.

Let me say a final word on Nathan’s concern about how LLMs/machine learning systems preserve or corrupt the provenance of arguments. I admitted above I have not had a chance to read his entire reply as closely as I’d like, since I just don’t have the time or energy given my other responsibilities to produce the overwhelmingly detailed textual reply that he (with help from his LLM) has provided in reply to my post. The obvious remedy would be to have my own LLM summarize and draft a reply for me. But I worry this would only multiply misunderstandings, and for reasons internal to our dispute itself. On the accounts I’ve offered in my recent monograph and elsewhere, LLMs traffic only in abstract statistical patterns of tokenized text and are constitutively blind to the felt inheritances that render linguistic meaning concretely intelligible. My point is not that this sort of abstraction cannot be tremendously useful in the right contexts. My point is that that sort of abstraction of felt meaning from structural patterning is precisely what we are debating. So I would rather respond selectively to what I’ve had time to review and digest than to manufacture the appearance of comprehensiveness by outsourcing the exchange to a system that is blind to precisely what we are disagreeing about.

What Whitehead cannot establish by restating Whitehead

Matt’s later formulation is useful because it states the Whiteheadian commitment directly:

“Concretely considered, causal relation just is felt inheritance of the past into the present.”

Good. That is the claim I have been trying to examine.

If Whitehead builds “to be actual is to feel and be felt” into his account of actuality, then of course subtracting feeling leaves no Whiteheadian actuality. But that shows what follows once the Whiteheadian identity is accepted. It does not independently establish the identity.

A doctrine cannot cite its own definition as evidence that the world is as the doctrine defines it.

Nor do I owe a replacement intrinsic ontology before I am allowed to withhold assent from Matt’s.

“I do not know what causality is intrinsically” is not answered by “then tell me what it is.”

Ignorance is not a vacancy notice for the first metaphysics willing to move in.

The question remains:

What gives someone outside the Whiteheadian scheme a reason to believe that concrete causal relation is felt inheritance rather than to understand that as one serious metaphysical interpretation of causal-relational reality?

Schelling gets us to the bridge, not across it

Matt’s appeal to Schelling gives him a serious argument about epistemic access. Embodied creatures encounter causal efficacy through feeling. That may tell us something important about how causation becomes concretely intelligible to us.

It does not yet establish that causal efficacy universally is feeling.

Matt effectively concedes this when he says Schelling does not by itself prove nature experiential.

Good. That concession gets us to the bridge. It does not cross it.

One known class of cases contains both causal efficacy and feeling. The remaining question is which features of those cases should be generalized, across which boundaries and scales, and why “felt inheritance” rather than the causal-relational structure shared by both accounts receives the additional evidential credit.

The Bridge I am actually proposing

The comparison is straightforward.

Preserve everything both accounts already have reason to accept: causal inheritance, relation, receptivity, integration, novelty, organization, real influence, embodied causal participation where established, and whatever else survives scrutiny.

Then ask what is specifically gained by identifying that process with feeling.

This does not assume that the distinction exists in nature. If the attempted separation itself produces a real explanatory, phenomenological, causal, predictive, or conceptual loss, Matt has evidence against my decomposition.

If Whitehead’s identification generates constraints or successes that the thinner process-relational rival cannot recover, show them. If every serious attempt to describe causal inheritance without experiential interpretation depends on experiential structure for reasons deeper than linguistic habit, establish that.

But the comparison has to be capable of returning the other result too. If the apparent work done by “feeling,” “prehension,” “valuation,” or “interiority” is already carried by structure both rivals accept, those shared successes cannot simply be counted again as evidence for the Whiteheadian interpretation.

Keep the common ground. Isolate the disputed remainder. Ask what earns it.

That is the Framework Bridge.

It does not require a scheme-neutral metaphysical language. Rival schemes can disagree about ontology while still facing shared observations, consequences, explanatory burdens, failures, counterexamples, interventions, and comparative standards. Matt himself appeals to coherence, adequacy, applicability, and avoidance of misplaced concreteness. The question is whether those standards actually discriminate his identity claim from the strongest rival that preserves the process-relational structure while leaving primitive intrinsic character unresolved.

If they do, show where.

If they do not, underdetermination is an available result.

The admission did not repair the public argument

This is where the handling of the misattribution matters.

If I had published an article to thousands of readers attributing to Matt a position he had explicitly left open, built a sustained critique around that position, and later discovered that I had collapsed the conditional structure of his argument, I would not consider a downstream acknowledgment sufficient.

I would put the correction where the error lives: at the top of the article, before another reader encountered the misattributed position as Matt’s own. I would correct or visibly mark the affected passages. I would identify which criticisms depended on the mistaken reconstruction and revise or withdraw them where necessary.

And if the original article had already been distributed to thousands of subscribers, I would send the correction through the same channel. The readers who received the error are the readers who need a realistic opportunity to receive the correction.

What happened instead leaves the asymmetry largely intact. The original article remained live with the disputed reconstruction doing its argumentative work. Readers could still encounter the misattributed position first, work through the critique of it, and reach the acknowledgment only afterward. Prior readers who had already received the original article were not thereby returned to the page and asked to update.

An erratum has no retroactive powers.

When the target changes, the downstream argument has to be re-audited.

Some criticisms may survive. Some may need reformulation. Some may no longer have a target. What cannot simply be assumed is that conceding the faulty premise leaves every dependent conclusion standing.

That is not punishment. It is provenance repair.

Why the asymmetry matters

This is not mainly a complaint about tone or embarrassment. It concerns how a public record becomes path-dependent.

Matt’s reconstruction reached thousands of readers before my correction could reach them. Some will never read my response. Some will never see his acknowledgment. Many will encounter his characterization before mine. A simpler caricature is also easier to remember than the conditional argument it replaced.

That matters especially for my work because the distinctions being collapsed are central to the methodology itself: conditional branching, provisional decomposition, symmetry between rivals, explicit agnosticism, and the separation of analytical tests from ontological commitments.

If “if this factorization succeeds” becomes “Nathan believes this factorization describes reality,” a feature designed to make the argument corrigible is converted into evidence of dogmatism.

If asking whether two constructs can be distinguished becomes asserting that they are ontologically separable, a method for testing ontology is made to look as though it assumes one.

If agnosticism about primitive intrinsic character becomes commitment to feelingless causality, readers can come away believing I defend the very binary I have been trying to interrogate.

The earlier article supplies that documentary record. What remains here is the distribution problem: the audience that received the mistake deserves a realistic opportunity to receive the repair.

The computational-workflow diversion does not answer any of this

Matt also turns from the attribution problem to speculation about my use of computational tools, suggesting that such systems traffic in abstract statistical patterns and may multiply misunderstanding or manufacture an appearance of comprehensiveness.

That description does not match the method that produced my response.

I bring the argument, the questions, the primary texts, the peer-reviewed literature, the quotations, the prior exchanges, and the framework I have spent decades developing. I use computational systems to help search that material, compare exact wording, retrieve sources, generate objections, test rival interpretations, expose weak inferences, sharpen distinctions, and make the prose clearer. I then reread the results, reject bad formulations, revise others, verify quotations and citations, and take responsibility for every claim I publish.

The point of the machinery is not to free me from engaging the source. It is to make it harder for either of us to get away from the source.

If that process corrupted my argument, there is a straightforward way to demonstrate it: quote the corrupted passage, show the source it misrepresents, and trace the faulty inference. I would want that criticism because I could correct the error.

Matt does not do that here. Instead, he moves from a general philosophical claim about what language models supposedly cannot experience to speculation about how my response was produced. That creates the appearance of addressing the epistemic reliability of my argument while bypassing the simpler textual question already documented in the record: did I actually argue what he attributed to me?

Whether the tool feels anything has no bearing on whether the word “if” was present in my sentence.

There is also a human consequence to this framing. The response Matt was discussing took many hours to assemble, check, revise, and document. Behind it are thousands of hours of study and framework-building across roughly thirty years. Whatever anyone ultimately thinks of that framework, it did not originate in a chatbot prompt. One reason I have worked to make it explicit enough to operationalize is precisely so that computational systems can apply its tests, expose its weaknesses, and help me reason through large bodies of material without replacing my judgment.

Reducing that coupled process to “outsourcing” therefore reverses the actual distribution of work. The computational system did not spend those decades developing the questions I am asking Matt. It did not choose the research programme, decide which sources mattered, determine which objections I accepted, reject its own bad outputs on my behalf, or authorize the claims I published. I did.

That framing also erases the labor that made computational assistance useful in the first place. A system can operationalize my framework because I spent years making the distinctions, questions, revision rules, and evidential standards explicit enough to be operationalized. The scaffold amplifies and tests that prior work; it does not retroactively become its author.

So when attention shifts from the documented source mismatch to assumptions about how I produced my response, the effect is not merely to criticize a tool. It functions to diminish my authorship and agency by treating the scaffold as though it were the thinker and the person directing, checking, revising, and taking responsibility for the process as though he were incidental to the result.

That has a dehumanizing effect regardless of intent: the human source of the questions, judgment, accumulated learning, and responsibility disappears behind the instrument used to extend them. Because I also use these tools partly to compensate for physical limitations, the framing can have an ableist effect as well.

But the methodological point does not depend on either concern. The validity of an argument is not determined by whether its author used a library, a search engine, a notebook, another person, a statistical model, or a computational interlocutor while developing it. The relevant questions are whether the sources are accurate, whether the inference follows, whether the provenance is preserved, and whether errors remain corrigible.

If Matt did not have time to read enough of a piece to know what position it defended, the responsible alternative was to withhold the attribution, not to write as though the reconstructed position were mine.

A correction should reduce the distance between the source and its public representation. It should not create a new route around the source.

I do not want a weaker version of Matt’s argument. I want the strongest criticism of what I actually argued.

Perhaps my factorization fails. Show me why.

Perhaps affectivity cannot be separated, even analytically, from causal efficacy without destroying something indispensable.

Perhaps embodied first-person access supplies a legitimate bridge from felt causation in living systems to the intrinsic character of causal relation generally.

If so, that is exactly what I want to know.

My alternative is much thinner:

Accept that causal relations are real, that the past genuinely influences the present, that systems receive and integrate influences, respond, organize, and generate novelty, while remaining agnostic about whether the primitive intrinsic character of those relations is literally feeling or experience.

For a philosopher, that is a process-relational account that preserves the shared causal structure while suspending judgment about its intrinsic character.

For everyone else: we can agree that real things really affect one another without pretending we already know what that interaction is “like from the inside,” if it is like anything at all.

That is the rival Matt’s identity claim has to beat.

Explaining what follows once “causal relation just is felt inheritance” has been accepted does not yet tell someone who has not accepted that identity why they should.

What differential warrant favors the identity claim that causal relation just is felt inheritance over the strongest process-relational rival that accepts causal inheritance, receptivity, integration, novelty, organization, and real influence while leaving primitive intrinsic character unresolved?

In plain English:

What does calling causation “feeling” explain, predict, constrain, or otherwise establish that we do not already get by accepting the real causal process while admitting that we do not yet know its intrinsic nature?

And this is where the present evidence matters.

We have abundant evidence for causal inheritance, organization, integration, responsiveness, and real influence. We also have strong evidence that the forms of consciousness we actually know depend systematically on particular kinds of organized biological activity.

What we do not presently have is evidence that clearly distinguishes “causal relation itself is felt inheritance” from “causal relation is real, while its primitive intrinsic character remains unresolved.”

That does not show that causation is feelingless.

It means the specifically experiential part of Matt’s universal claim has not yet been independently isolated from the causal-relational structure both accounts already accept.

So the thinner position has the present evidential advantage for a very modest reason: it stops where the discrimination stops.

But it can lose.

My position should lose ground if attempts to bracket feeling repeatedly destroy something indispensable to causal explanation, if experiential concepts generate novel and successful predictions or constraints that the thinner account cannot recover, or if some other severe comparison shows that supposedly neutral causal structure is only intelligible by covertly importing the experiential character Matt claims is fundamental.

Matt’s position should lose ground if causal inheritance, receptivity, integration, novelty, and real influence continue to do the relevant explanatory work while “felt inheritance” contributes no independently discriminating success, or if a rival account explains the supposed experiential residual more successfully without universalizing feeling to causal relation as such.

That gives both positions somewhere to go.

I understand what Whitehead says.

I am still asking why I should believe him.

And at this point the dispute can be reduced to two very simple questions.

What could we learn, observe, argue, or explain that would make you lower your confidence that causal relation itself is felt inheritance?

In plain English: what could make you say, “Causation is real, but I was wrong to identify causation itself with feeling”?

If nothing could, in what sense is the claim fallible?

Matt calls the proposal fallible and comparatively warranted. So there should be some possible evidential, explanatory, conceptual, or comparative result that counts against it.

If no discovery, argument, explanatory failure, or superior rival could ever lower confidence in the identity claim because “actuality” already means “felt actuality” inside the scheme, then the problem becomes straightforward:

What part of the claim is being discovered about reality, and what part has simply been secured by definition?

My alternative does not say causation is feelingless.

It says we do not yet know that causation is feeling.

The evidence presently establishes the causal-relational structure far more securely than either universal answer about its intrinsic character.

So both sides should be able to answer the same question:

What would make your position lose?

Mine loses if feeling proves indispensable in ways the thinner account cannot recover.

Matt’s loses ground if the shared causal-relational structure keeps doing the work while the universal identification with feeling never earns a discriminating success of its own.

And if nothing could ever count against that identification, what exactly are we being asked to believe beyond the definition itself?
