Bubble Sort Doesn’t Prove God: The Discovery Institute Recognizes an Opening in Levin’s Platonic Interpretation

In short: Michael Levin's bubble sort result is better understood through classical algorithm theory than as a revelation about Platonic space, and the Discovery Institute has recognized the opening that interpretation creates for God-of-the-gaps arguments. Christopher Hitchens would have eviscerated this bubble sort argument in three sentences, but I’ll need more space because the inferential overreach here is layered like a particularly overripe onion. Michael Levin, whose legitimate work on bioelectricity deserves respect, and his collaborators report unexpected collective behaviors in distributed sorting systems built from familiar policies. Those findings deserve analysis. He connects them to Platonic space; the connection is what needs evidence.

Treating surprise as proof of an independent source is a category error wrapped in breathless mysticism and sold as profundity, and the “beyond the algorithm” argument risks the same inferential move as intelligent design when it credits an additional source without distinguishing it from the implemented system (local rules, inputs, scheduling, hardware, etc.).

The title names a pattern of reasoning. Levin rejects intelligent design and agrees that the sorting behavior follows the implemented rules. My criticism applies when an unanticipated result, the absence of an explicit clustering goal, or the failure of one proposed mechanism is credited as support for an independent Platonic source. Those facts do not identify that source. Mathematical constraint, a useful behavioral model and an independently causally active pattern are different claims.

The most consequential distinction deserves stating plainly: validating a biological finding is not the same achievement as validating the proposed source of that finding. Joel’s own assessment awards nonphysical ingress no earned credit. Averaging support for other claims cannot supply that missing support. Even if every biological effect listed here were independently replicated, those replications would establish the effects more securely; they would support the additional Platonic contribution only insofar as their design and results distinguished it from serious alternatives. Where independent corroboration remains unestablished, that uncertainty belongs beside the particular finding. The task is not the complete algorithm

There are several different objects being called “the algorithm”: the instruction to sort numerical values; the actual local policies, state variables, scheduling and stopping rules; and the trajectory produced by those rules from a particular starting state. The sorting goal does not specify every feature of that trajectory. A complete implementation can determine features that its designer never listed as goals.

In the Lex Fridman clip, Levin accepts that someone could track the clustering through the algorithm. He then describes the additional behavior as “neither prescribed nor forbidden by the algorithm” (14:46–15:28 in the clip; 02:18:32 onward in the full interview). That wording needs a stable referent. Clustering may be unspecified by the sorting task while remaining a consequence of the complete implemented dynamics. Moving between those senses makes a consequence of the rules sound as though it occupies a causal vacancy between them.

Write the implemented update schematically as x(t + 1) = F(x(t), u(t)), where x includes cell values, types and internal state, and u includes the scheduled choices and any random input. Once these are fixed, the model fixes the trajectory. Aggregation is another function of that state, A(t) = g(x(t)). There need be no instruction saying “maximize g” for g to rise. This does not make every consequence obvious, easy to prove, or computationally cheap to predict.

The published study makes a related inference when it says clustering is “not something that can be predicted a priori,” because cells do not read algotype information (Section 4.4). But type already determines the policy a cell executes. Policy affects its movements; movements affect who becomes adjacent. The causal route can run through behavior without a direct query for a neighbor’s type. Absence of such a query does not establish an impossibility of prediction. The authors themselves investigate an efficiency-based explanation. Sorting paper.

This is why the six-line textbook routine cannot serve as a complete specification of the mixed experiment. The paper openly describes the distributed architecture; Levin also explains that change in the Lex interview. The criticism concerns what follows from that architecture, rather than a suggestion that the authors concealed it.

There is a further reason to inspect the implementation before accepting broad claims about the absence of defect handling. In the authors’ public repository, the Insertion cell’s prefix check explicitly tests for CellStatus.FREEZE, resets its comparison value, and skips that cell. An isolated execution of that method allows movement with the left prefix [3, frozen 1]; removing the freeze-specific branch blocks it. The pinned swap routine blocks a frozen cell from initiating a swap, while the Bubble and Insertion policies permit other cells to swap with it. A passive movable cell and an immovable wall are different experimental conditions. So are “no explicit repair planner” and “no implemented response to a defective cell.”

Clustering formation and clustering persistence are different questions

My original stability explanation conflated them. A globally stable sort preserves the starting relative order of every equal-key record. Strict comparisons alone do not guarantee that property in a mixed system containing nonadjacent swaps. In [2a, 2b, 1c], swapping the first and last records produces [1c, 2b, 2a]. The equal records have reversed without being directly swapped with each other.

In the replay of Joel’s model, every one of the 90 duplicate-key runs reversed some equal-key records. The mixed model therefore lacks the global stability my complete explanation would require. Clustering with unique values also needs an explanation independent of equal-key preservation. Those objections to my old account stand.

But duplicate keys still loosen the final ordering constraint. Different policies can create segregation during sorting, and strict tie handling can help retain it when numerical sorting is complete. Continuing the actual final cells with their existing internal states and unchanged strict rules produced no further swaps in all 105 replayed runs. This establishes persistence at those completed endpoints in the tested implementation.

At ten copies per key, the mixed model’s mean final aggregation was 0.6592. Applying a globally stable numerical sort to the same starting records instead gave 0.503333. Uniformly permuting algotypes only within the final equal-value blocks, while preserving each block’s type counts, gave an exact conditional mean of 0.485117. Numerical sortedness survives this last operation while much of the measured segregation does not. The arrangement within tied blocks matters.

These comparisons distinguish formation from retention. They do not establish strict tie handling as the unique necessary mechanism, and the within-block permutation is not Joel’s separate experiment allowing equal-key swaps. Nor is replaying his sequential model an independent replication of the original threaded implementation. The November 1 audit reports separate runs using the upstream cell policies; their finite observation windows and instrumentation are described below. What survives my first explanation is this narrower role in persistence. The complete stability explanation remains withdrawn. Pinned model and experiment.

What Joel’s “flat 0.50 prediction” gets right, and what it assumes

Neither version of my article stated the flat 0.50 numerical prediction Joel attributes to me. That does not prevent a critic deriving a consequence from an explanation I offered. It does require distinguishing my words from his reconstruction and supplying the assumptions the reconstruction needs.

Joel’s working draft offers a rationale: if global stability merely preserved the initially random type order among equal-key records, final aggregation should remain at chance across duplicate multiplicities. A completed globally stable sort, with uniformly randomized type labels independent of the numerical-key arrangement, supports that endpoint benchmark. But stability is not necessary for the benchmark: any completed sort whose output permutation is independent of those randomized labels has the same expectation, even if it reverses equal-key records. A systematic departure under that randomization can therefore reveal type-dependent ordering; it does not diagnose global instability specifically. Equal-key reversals test that separate property directly. The benchmark can still challenge my stronger old account when its assumptions apply. “It never directly swaps equal keys” does not establish the global property that account needed. The peer-reviewed definition of stable sorting concerns preservation of equal-key relative order throughout the final output.

In Joel’s figure, “flat” refers to final aggregation plotted against copies per key, not to aggregation remaining constant at every moment during sorting. I do not attribute the latter prediction to that figure. An endpoint argument would not establish such a trajectory, but that limitation does not answer his actual comparison across duplicate multiplicities. My objections concern attributing the numerical forecast to my published words and carrying a successful objection to the old explanation over to the corrected argument.

For a linear array with exactly b labels of one type and s of another, let N = b + s ≥ 2, let H count matching neighboring pairs, and define aggregation as A = H/N, the measure used in Joel’s code. Uniform random arrangement gives:

E[A] = [b(b − 1) + s(s − 1)]/N².

There are N − 1 neighboring pairs. Each matches with probability [b(b − 1) + s(s − 1)]/[N(N − 1)]; dividing the expected matching-pair count by N gives the expression above. With equal fixed counts, this becomes E[A] = 1/2 − 1/N. For Joel’s 40/40, 80-cell experiment, it is 0.4875, rather than exactly 0.50. A denominator of N − 1, or independent coin-flip labels rather than fixed counts, gives a different finite-size answer. The same expectation follows from the classical runs-count identity: if R is the number of contiguous same-type runs, then H = N − R. This is a standard runs-statistic baseline, not a distinctive prediction of a stable algorithm.

The calculation was checked using exact rational arithmetic, independent C++ and Python enumeration, a recurrence over runs, symbolic simplification and seeded simulation. It supplies a conditional mathematical consequence. Joel does not need to establish that the actual mixed sort is globally stable before using it to test an explanation that says it is: departures from the benchmark and observed equal-key reversals can count against that explanation. The same stable-sort benchmark remains constant in expectation as duplicate multiplicity changes under these assumptions. Individual realized samples need not have identical scores.

The correction to 0.4875 does not rescue my old explanation from the large clustering effect. And it would be a mistake to dismiss Joel’s two-copy result merely because 0.4767 lies less than one reported standard error below that unconditional expectation. A more specific exploratory test fixes the observed type counts within every equal-value block and randomizes their internal order. Across the 15 two-copy runs, the observed total was 572 matching pairs, against a conditional expectation of 603.5. Exact counting gave a two-sided distance-from-expectation tail probability of approximately 0.00201; a separate 100,000-draw simulation gave 0.00208. That supports an ordering bias relative to this conditional reference. The comparison was chosen after inspecting the results. The November 1 archive reports follow-up tests on new inputs in the original threaded code. Those reported results strengthen the case for this ordering bias if their implementation and conditional null withstand checking; they do not retroactively preregister the exploratory comparison.

Joel therefore has a valid objection to an appropriately specified complete stability account. Making its randomization assumptions and finite-size expectation explicit improves its precision. It remains a consequence he reconstructed, rather than a numerical forecast I published; the two experimental conditions are not two separately stated predictions of mine. The objection also leaves a different possibility open: movement policies can form clustering which tie handling subsequently helps retain. Rejecting my admittedly incomplete mechanism does not eliminate that narrower possibility or identify an independent Platonic cause. A gap in current understanding doesn't necessarily imply a non-physical source; it implies more work needs to be done.

What the reductionism charge would need to establish

Joel's reductionist framing of my argument raises a related issue to my earlier exchange with Levin, although the two writers do not make identical accusations. The fuller exchange is discussed in my companion article on Levin’s response. The comparison concerns their representations of the argument; it establishes neither copying nor deliberate deception.

In my October 30, 2025 symposium comment, I explicitly included higher-level causal explanation in a naturalistic rival offered for comparison, citing the possibility that macro-level descriptions “can have higher effective information than micro-level descriptions.” Levin nevertheless answered a sentence about physical causal constitution with: “That’s a reductionist argument against the reality of other causal levels.” On the specific meaning he supplied, that attribution is demonstrably contradicted by the surrounding text: the rival I offered expressly included those levels. My physicalist wording could be challenged, and my proposed mechanisms could be wrong, without turning that proposal into a denial of higher-level causation. On November 11 he acknowledged his initial physicalist reading and asked me to clarify my alternative, a reasonable request. My comment; his October reply; his November clarification.

“Reductionism” covers several different claims. The Stanford Encyclopedia’s account distinguishes reduction from elimination: explaining a phenomenon through its constituents need not deny its reality. Its account of reductionism in biology distinguishes ontological, epistemic and methodological reduction, which do not simply entail one another. Offering a naturalistic explanation does not, by itself, commit me to every position collected under that label.

The causal-emergence literature gives this distinction a concrete setting. In my exchanges with Levin, I cited Hoel, Albantakis and Tononi to show how a naturalistic rival could retain higher-level causal explanation without requiring supernatural, nonphysical or, in Levin’s terminology, “extra-natural” causation. Their paper and Hoel’s later treatment compare causal models whose macro descriptions can outperform their micro descriptions under a specified information measure. Their results depend on how scales and interventions are defined; Eberhardt and Lee challenge aspects of that construction. Those papers do not establish my proposed thermodynamic account or settle the ontology of causation. My proposed rival requires a place for genuine higher-level causal organization of the kind this literature investigates. Whether a particular system satisfies Hoel’s precise criterion remains a modeling question. My quotations establish that I offered such an account for comparison; they do not establish that its mechanisms succeed. My central request for warrant for an additional Platonic source also survives a revision or failure of that particular causal-emergence formalism.

If the objection is methodological, my comparisons do not require that the smallest scale always supplies the best explanation. I offered thermodynamic constraint dynamics, organizational closure and higher-level causal organization for assessment. If the objection is explanatory, I did not argue that finding a mechanism makes mathematical, functional or collective descriptions dispensable. Explaining how a pattern is produced can coexist with explaining its constraints and organization. Nothing in this sorting critique requires minds, agency or consciousness to be exhausted by a computational description either. These stronger commitments would need to be demonstrated from my argument, rather than inferred from my request for a naturalistic comparison.

Joel’s clearest stated target is “reductionist dismissals that wave away real emergent phenomena without checking the math.” Offering an explanation is not waving a phenomenon away. It takes the phenomenon seriously enough to ask how it occurs and test the answer. My February claim that textbook stability already supplied the complete explanation was overconfident, and Joel has a substantive objection to its adequacy. I own that error. Whether that local explanation succeeds does not decide my position on ontological, methodological or explanatory reductionism. An inadequate explanation does not establish denial of the phenomenon, and correcting it does not establish an independent Platonic source. The October 13 revision, preceding his October 25 invitation, explicitly withdrew the complete mechanism and acknowledged potentially novel results. Calling that corrected position dismissive requires showing what finding or explanatory possibility it actually dismisses.

An ontological reading needs a different answer. Under some philosophical taxonomies, my earlier physicalist wording could reasonably be classified as reductionist even while retaining higher-level explanations. I do not need to win that classification dispute for the causal criticism to hold. Offering a rival that requires no nonphysical source does not prove that no such source exists. Equally, attaching a label to the rival does not show that the proposed extra source contributes anything. My remaining question requires no premise that naturalism is the final, exhaustive ontology.

If the concern is that I exclude an unconventional source before considering it, the November 1 public repository version quotes my comparison proposal from Religion with a Pipette. The audit traces it to that October 4 article and reports a change of subject from his symposium talk to Joel himself; the excerpt also stops before my explicit application of the same standard to naturalistic rivals. Those qualifications matter, but the comparative test is visibly present in his own text. I asked what contribution could earn support through independently justified explanatory, predictive or interventional gains. That supplies a route to reconsideration; neither compatibility with the result nor the failure of one rival supplies those gains by itself. Joel also distinguishes the sufficiency of the dynamics from the falsity of a further source. I make that distinction too. Whether a particular naturalistic rival succeeds remains a separate question from whether the additional causal contribution has been demonstrated.

Across these readings, the charge needs an argument of its own. Denying higher-level causation contradicts the rival I offered. Privileging the smallest scale, eliminating useful explanations, or ruling out alternatives in advance does not follow from it. A defensible technical classification might still apply, but that would not answer the causal question. Joel’s historical objection to my complete stability explanation survives; the wider dismissal of my corrected criticism does not follow from that local objection. Explaining emergence is legitimate work. So is asking what evidence warrants crediting an additional source.

“Free compute” needs a specified bill

In the Lex clip, 13:06–13:40, Levin moves from clustering requiring no extra clustering instructions to the possibility of useful ingressions without extra physical costs. In the Curt Jaimungal clip, 2:16–2:35, he presents free compute as a testable prediction. There is a legitimate engineering question here: can one process provide several useful outputs for less cost than computing each separately?

Michael Levin discusses sorting algorithms with Curt Jaimungal and Anil Seth.

If “free” means that the already-running process yields an additional useful pattern without a dedicated extra clustering routine, the observation can support that modest claim. Reusing work is useful. It needs no exemption from physics and supplies no identification of a nonphysical contributor.

A stronger efficiency claim needs a comparison. Fix the required outputs and their accuracy, the inputs and type assignment, and the implementation boundary. Then compare the mixed sort with serious ways of obtaining the same outputs, counting comparisons, movements, scheduling, memory, measurement and readout where relevant. If the claim concerns energy, measure or model the relevant physical costs. A procedure that explicitly recognizes types and commands clustering is one possible rival; its expense is not a lower bound on every mechanism that could produce the pattern.

Landauer’s principle adds no universal charge for every bit merely being present or for every comparison and swap. It constrains logically irreversible information loss under specified conditions. Conversely, failing to add a purpose-specific instruction does not demonstrate zero additional physical cost. The same trace can yield two observables without the second observable opening a hole in the energy budget. Landauer; Bennett.

Levin explicitly disclaims breaking physics in the Curt discussion. The issue is therefore the inference from a potentially useful shared computation to causal ingress, together with the unmeasured physical-cost claim. A measured advantage would deserve engineering credit. Its proposed Platonic source would still need separate support.

A rising score does not yet establish a detour

Joel’s distinction between two sorting measures is useful, provided neither is promoted into physical energy without justification. Let I(v) count all inverted pairs and E(v) count adjacent descents. In his example:

Swapping the adjacent inverted pair 4, 1 improves I while worsening E. Every strict adjacent inversion swap lowers I by exactly one; only that pair’s relative order changes. Both the example and this property were checked computationally, including exhaustive checks through eight distinct keys. The audit archive supplies a general Lean proof for adjacent swaps. Long-range inverted-pair swaps can lower I by more than one. With distinct keys the decrement is odd; with repeated keys it need not be: [2, 2, 1] → [1, 2, 2] removes two inversions. A temporary decline in the paper’s adjacent-order score can therefore occur without frozen cells, anticipation, or a move uphill in I.

That does not make E an illegitimate observable. Nor does a mathematical Lyapunov function become the system’s thermodynamic energy merely by being called its “real energy.” The paper also compares behavior across defect conditions; an example without defects does not dispose of that comparative result. It does establish that a dip-and-recovery curve alone cannot distinguish obstacle-sensitive problem solving from the projection of ordinary sorting dynamics onto this score. An adequate comparison must test the defect response beyond that baseline and distinguish movable passive cells from genuinely immovable barriers. With a genuinely immovable middle cell, [2, frozen 1, 0] has three inversions but no legal adjacent swap. A no-defect convergence argument cannot simply be carried across that change in the allowed moves.

The published study uses a minimal behavioral conception of intelligence, and explicitly separates its target from first-person affect or experience. The relevant question is whether its behavioral criteria establish the capacities claimed. Higher-level descriptions may earn predictive or interventionist value even when the dynamics are fully implemented. Demonstrating such value would support those descriptions; it would not, by itself, establish felt motivation or a separately acting nonphysical mind.

What the sorting results would establish

Both audit reports reproduce the stated deterministic values from my October 31 replay of Joel’s sequential model. Only the Claude audit reports the follow-up runs using the upstream threaded cell policies. That distinction matters: matching sequential ports does not establish swap-for-swap equivalence with operating-system threads. The new reports should be judged at the boundary actually tested.

The threaded runs were instrumented and stopped after a finite no-swap window. No swap for a second and a half is evidence of observed quiescence, not a proof that movement can never resume. The reported new-input results also remain conditional on the test’s reference distribution. The archive includes null simulations and passive-label controls; one passive-label comparison returned a small p-value. Those diagnostics need assessment together, including sampling uncertainty and the comparisons attempted. I would not turn a finite calibration exercise into a declaration that the test cannot mislead.

These checks can strengthen a mechanism-level account of formation and retention. They also support parts of Joel’s empirical description. Explaining emergence takes the phenomenon seriously; it does not make it disappear. A mistaken proposed mechanism calls for correction, not an inference that the critic denies higher-level causation. My comparison expressly included higher-level causal organization. Neither its computational success nor its failure settles the independent Platonic contribution.

A training corpus is not the whole research system

Joel states the confinement claim explicitly:

In that sense, trained on previous corpora of human knowledge and without phenomenological awareness, the AI remains self-referential with that corpus. Of “within” and “without,” the AI is forced to be “within,” because that is all that it knows.

Joel’s draft moves from limitations of training data to the claim that AI is confined to that corpus and structurally unable to encounter what lies outside it. That conclusion leaves out the system actually doing the work. A pretrained model, its current context, supplied documents, retrieval tools, code execution, experimental observations and human review are different parts of a research workflow. Information can enter the workflow without changing the model’s trained weights. Whether it is then interpreted correctly is another question.

The mechanism is ordinary input conditioning. Learned parameters supply capacities and prior tendencies; the current context supplies the particular question, documents and returned tool results. During ordinary inference, new context can change the response without changing the trained parameters. Updating an answer from a retrieved paper is different from retraining the model. Saving a result in an external notebook is different again. None of these distinctions requires treating the model as a conscious observer or granting it infallible understanding.

An agent can request a document, receive its text, generate code, run that code through a tool, and receive an error or result as subsequent input. The surrounding software performs the retrieval or execution. The model can then respond to information that was not supplied by its weights alone. A DOI lookup can contradict a proposed citation; a test execution can contradict generated code. These channels can be corrupted, ignored or misunderstood, but they are not merely the model consulting its training corpus.

There is newer peer-reviewed evidence than the early ReAct study. Lu and colleagues’ ToolSandbox, published in Findings of NAACL 2025, evaluates stateful tool execution, dependencies between operations and conversational feedback. It reports substantial remaining difficulties, including handling insufficient information. The relevant object of evaluation is an interacting system, not an isolated text completion.

Jin and colleagues’ Search-R1, accepted at COLM 2025, trains models to interleave reasoning with search queries and retrieved passages. Its question-answering experiments demonstrate a concrete route for using external information. Reinforcement learning develops the search policy during training; returned passages subsequently enter the inference context. The study does not establish reliable open-ended scientific discovery, and its results do not prove that every retrieved passage was absent from pretraining. The narrower point is sufficient: inference need not depend on trained parameters alone.

The caution is equally current. Cho and Park’s Skill Following, published in Findings of EMNLP 2026, compares matched skill-enabled and skill-disabled executions. It reports cases where favorable aggregate retrieval comparisons conceal harmful effects on the tasks where retrieval actually occurred. Receiving information and using it correctly are different achievements. That finding supports testing what each part of my workflow contributes; it does not license declaring it reliable merely because papers and tools were supplied.

The literature therefore supports a specific correction to Joel’s explanation: training influences what a model does, but does not seal the whole workflow off from additional inputs. It also supplies a warning against my overclaiming. To establish that a source, tool or referee improved an audit, inspect its actual effect or compare appropriately matched runs. Neither unconditional trust nor unconditional confinement describes the evidence.

Later, his account of the research process says:

Every node there is pinned to something outside the corpus—a wet-lab dataset, a held-out species, a hornet wing—so when a pre-registered prediction fails, the failure comes from nature rather than from the hivemind.

These passages describe different boundaries. The first confines the AI to its corpus; the second describes a workflow receiving corrective information from outside it. They can be reconciled by distinguishing the pretrained model from the larger system. That distinction also has to be allowed when assessing my work.

Joel’s own method makes the point. He describes held-out organisms, external datasets, human direction, executable tests and cross-provider review. Those are possible routes for correcting a model’s inherited expectations. They are available to criticism as well as endorsement. If my use of those routes fails, identify the failure. A description of an isolated model cannot establish the limitations of the whole coupled workflow.

Encountering new information does not guarantee recognizing its significance or discovering something new. But that uncertainty is different from being unable to receive it. A locked hypothesis supplies a goal for an investigation; it does not establish a metaphysical escape from computation. Calling this an exit from the corpus concedes that the surrounding workflow matters.

The same standard applies to people. Human experience is mediated by perception, language, instruments and prior learning. None supplies immunity from error. Differences in embodiment can matter, but their relevance to a particular inference needs to be shown.

What Bubble Sort Stability Explains

Computer scientists have a term for duplicate-key order preservation. It’s called algorithm stability, and it’s been documented in standard algorithms textbooks for decades.

A stable sorting algorithm preserves the relative order of elements with equal keys. When you sort [5, 3, 5, 1] and the two 5s end up adjacent to each other in their original relative order, that adjacency follows numerical sorting; their retained relative order is stability. That’s bubble sort doing exactly what bubble sort does, mechanically and deterministically, because ordinary stable bubble sort swaps adjacent elements only when they are strictly out of order.

Donald Knuth’s The Art of Computer Programming (1973) discusses stability. Thomas Cormen’s Introduction to Algorithms (1990) also discusses sorting and stability. Equal-key order preservation is undergraduate computer science. That familiarity does not establish that the mixed system’s particular algotype-clustering curves were already explained.

When equal elements retain their original relative order, that is stability. This is not evidence of an additional agency. This is not evidence of felt intrinsic motivation. This is what happens when a stable comparison-based sorting algorithm encounters equal keys; algotype clustering is a different question.

When you grant each element in a sorting array autonomous local decision-making capacity, you have changed the sorting architecture. You have built an agent-based model whose collective behavior still needs analysis. This is rather like announcing that you have found a new source of fire, neglecting to mention that you are standing in a room full of fireplaces, and declining to explain what the fireplaces leave for that source to explain.

Thomas Schelling, an economist, not a biologist, and therefore perhaps less susceptible to the temptations of Platonic mysticism, demonstrated in 1969, working with coins on a checkerboard, that agents following simple local preference rules can cluster into segregated groups as a consequence of local rules and starting conditions. He published the formal results in the American Economic Review in 1969 and the Journal of Mathematical Sociology in 1971. The result did not require conscious coins or a separate nonphysical supplier. He observed that local rules produce global clustering, wrote it up, and moved on.

The model demonstrated that large-scale segregation patterns emerge even when no individual agent desires segregation and even when the threshold for similarity preference is quite modest. The field that grew from this work, with its roots in von Neumann’s cellular automata from the early 1950s and the first formal simulation frameworks arriving by the mid-1960s, spent the subsequent half-century investigating this explanatory family: simple local rules can produce complex emergent clustering through their interactions and starting conditions. No additional agency is established by clustering alone. No independent Platonic cause is thereby established. No breathless announcement to the audience of a popular podcast required.

Reynolds showed with the Boids model that coherent group motion can arise from a few simple rules operating on local information alone. Conway showed it with the Game of Life. Wolfram devoted an entire book to it. The Santa Fe Institute has been publishing papers about it since before some of its current fellows were born. The observation that agents following different local strategies self-organize into clusters belongs to a familiar explanatory family. A particular clustering curve can still be a finding; familiarity with emergence does not derive it. Giving that result an independent nonphysical source is roughly equivalent to crediting water flowing downhill to an extra agency before showing what gravity leaves unexplained.

What concerns the honest observer is not surprise itself, which is how inquiry finds something worth explaining. What concerns the honest observer is surprise receiving the credit of a metaphysical discovery. The appropriate question to ask — “when you converted the sorting algorithm into an agentic system, did you not change the system being studied?” — is not difficult. Lex Fridman did raise the possibility of modeling and proving the clustering, and Levin agreed that the algorithm could be traced. The harder question survives: what does the observation establish beyond those implemented dynamics?

The mystery, to adapt a phrase, is not in the algorithm. The mystery is in the willingness to let wonder outrun the inquiry needed for causal attribution.

Implementation Questions Require Evidence

Claims about what the algorithm “does” need to distinguish the abstract rule, the distributed model, and its execution. The following are implementation questions, not demonstrated causes of the measured clustering.

Modern compilers perform aggressive optimizations. Where a compiler generates machine code from bubble sort, it may:

Unroll loops for performance

Reorder operations to exploit instruction-level parallelism

Use SIMD instructions to compare multiple elements simultaneously

Generate code executed on processors using branch prediction and speculative execution

Generate code whose execution interacts with cache hierarchies and timing

None of this is “mysterious.” None of this is “between the lines.” All of it is documented in compiler design textbooks and processor architecture manuals. Correct optimizations preserve defined program semantics; those mechanisms do not automatically explain logical algotype clustering.

If Levin observed behaviors that seemed to deviate from his expectations, one question worth asking is: “What optimizations is the compiler performing?” Another question is: “What are the hardware effects?” The central question is: “Did I understand what the algorithm actually does?”

Levin explicitly accepts that the sorting behavior can be traced through the algorithm. The question is why the same behavior should then establish a separate Platonic contribution. Naming hardware effects does not answer that question; neither does naming Platonic space.

The Thermodynamic Reality Still Applies

Here’s what actually constrains and shapes algorithmic behavior, without establishing an independent Platonic supplier:

Energy dissipation: Physical sorting has implementation-dependent energetic costs. Landauer’s principle concerns logically irreversible erasure under specified conditions (kT ln 2 for an unbiased bit in the standard setting), not every comparison or swap. A swap can be logically reversible; counting comparisons and swaps alone does not determine dissipated joules.

Hardware constraints: The algorithm executes on physical substrates with real constraints: transistor switching times, wire delays, power limits, thermal budgets. These constraints shape execution at the implementation level; their contribution to a particular recorded pattern needs examination.

Implementation details: The programming language, compiler optimization level, processor architecture, memory hierarchy, and operating system scheduler all affect observable behavior. None of these are “mysterious.” All are documented.

Comparison function semantics: What counts as “equal” depends on how the comparison function is defined. Change the comparison function, and sorting behavior can change. Algotype clustering also depends on interactions among local policies and execution order. Executing those policies does not, by itself, identify an independent Platonic source.

Degrees of Freedom and the Source Claim

Levin makes much of his “degrees of freedom” argument, comparing algorithms to steganography, where you can hide information in bits that don’t affect the primary image. This analogy does not establish an independent source.

In steganography, you deliberately exploit redundancy to encode information. The “hidden” information exists because you put it there intentionally. In Levin’s bubble sort example, an additional observable pattern need not have been deliberately encoded. It can arise through interactions among the implemented rules. An extra useful output does not, by itself, require extra instructions or a nonphysical supplier.

When Levin allowed duplicate values to ease the sorting constraint, he created genuine degrees of freedom in the final arrangement of equal-valued cells. Those cells could remain grouped by algotype while the values were correctly sorted. Different policies can form segregation while strict tie handling can help retain it. That partial mechanism is distinct from global sorting stability. The endpoint tests support persistence in the tested model; they do not establish strict tie handling as its unique necessary mechanism or identify an independent nonphysical cause.

This is like noticing that when you remove constraints from a physical system, it explores more of its phase space, then concluding that the system must have “intrinsic motivation.” No. A system’s allowed trajectories depend on its transition rules and physical constraints. Relax a restriction, and more arrangements may become possible. That permits a phenomenon worth studying; it does not select a nonphysical explanation.

The Platonic Bait-and-Switch

Here’s the inferential move that needs scrutiny in sorting and biological examples:

  1. Observe a phenomenon you did not expect
  2. Describe its unexpected competence
  3. Suggest an independent Platonic source
  4. Use the usefulness of the research framework to motivate the source
  5. Still owe evidence distinguishing causal ingression from an effective heuristic
  6. Repeat

The experiments are science. Without that discriminating step, the additional cause is metaphysics wearing the experiments’ lab coat.

The bubble sort “discovery” is particularly revealing because unlike biological systems (where complexity can hide mechanistic gaps), the modified sorting model has published rules and code. What the Evidence Establishes

Here is what the mechanisms and evidence establish, without pretending to have derived every clustering curve:

Bubble sort stability: Ordinary adjacent bubble sort with strict inversion swaps preserves equal-key relative order. Merely refusing direct equal-key swaps does not guarantee global stability in a mixed system with nonadjacent swaps. Formation of algotype segregation and retention within tied blocks remain separate questions.

Compiler effects: High-level code is transformed by the compiler into machine code that may exhibit behaviors not obvious from reading the source. Correct optimizations preserve defined semantics; their role in this recorded clustering would require evidence.

Hardware effects: Modern processors reorder instructions, predict branches, and exploit parallelism in ways that create observable patterns. All of this is documented in processor manuals.

Thermodynamic constraints: Physical execution requires physical resources. Landauer’s bound applies to the relevant irreversible information operations, not every operation indiscriminately. Those constraints do not supply a derivation of algotype clustering.

Higher-level explanation: Describing collective organization, mathematical constraints or causal patterns at a useful scale can be legitimate explanatory work. Demonstrating a mechanism need not make those descriptions dispensable. Their success also does not, on its own, identify a further independently acting Platonic source.

No independent source established: The study and Levin’s interview acknowledge that the behavior follows the rules. An additional output can arise without an additional explicit goal. My positive mechanism claim required its own evidence; my request for the proposed source’s evidential warrant does not depend on that mechanism succeeding. Failure of my explanation does not identify his added source. Nor does an account that omits that source prove its universal absence.

Mysticism Is Not Discovery

Michael Levin’s sorting demonstration establishes unexpected collective behavior. It does not establish that an independent nonphysical pattern supplied it. The policies, starting conditions, and execution are available for investigation; the extra cause still needs its own evidential receipt.

This is not how science works. This is how gap-filling works.

Christopher Hitchens might have asked Levin one simple question: “What observation would count against your proposed cause?” Levin’s later paper does offer conditions for abandoning its research programme. The sharper question is whether a test distinguishes independent Platonic causation from a useful model or an intelligent-design reinterpretation of the same observations. A productive framework can earn its keep without its preferred ontology inheriting the result.

The sorting results expose limits in what we have characterized about these machines. They justify further investigation. When that finding is offered as evidence for an independent nonphysical cause, the appropriate response is to ask what establishes the proposed source beyond the implemented dynamics and serious alternatives.

The mystery isn’t in the algorithm. The mystery is why the extra cause should get the discovery’s credit.
